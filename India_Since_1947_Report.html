A plain-language report

India Since 1947: Every Government, What It Faced, What It Did, and What Changed

India became free at midnight on 15 August 1947 as one of the poorest countries on earth. Since then, it has been run by fourteen prime ministers across eighteen general elections. This report walks through each government in order: what it inherited, the conditions it worked under, the choices it made, what went right and wrong, and where it left the country. Every technical word is explained in simple language the first time it appears, and in the glossary.

How to read this report

Think of a country as a very large household. It earns money, spends money, borrows, saves, argues about who gets what, deals with neighbours, and raises its children. Almost every "difficult" political or economic word in this report is just a name for one of those everyday things. When you meet a dotted underlined word, you can click it to jump to the glossary, or just keep reading: the sentence around it explains the idea.

Each government gets its own chapter with the same shape, so you can compare them fairly:

  • At a glance: who, when, which party, and whether the government had a full majority or depended on partners.
  • What it inherited: the starting state of the country on the day the prime minister took the oath.
  • The conditions it worked under: wars, droughts, oil prices, the Cold War, world recessions, pandemics. No government chooses its weather.
  • What it did: politics and institutions, economy, society and religion, foreign policy and security, culture and science.
  • Ups and downs: the clear wins and the clear failures.
  • Could it have done better? Could it have been worse? A fair look at the choices that were available at the time, and what the government actually chose.
  • Where it left India, and what changed after: the ending state, and the consequences that showed up later, sometimes decades later.
A note on fairness

Judging governments is hard because they inherit different situations. A government that grew the economy at 4% a year in the 1960s, with no money, bad harvests and two wars, may have done a harder job than one that grew at 8% in the 2000s on a worldwide boom. This report tries to show the "weather" each government faced before judging its "driving". It also shows both the admirers' case and the critics' case for the most argued-about decisions (the Emergency, the 1991 reforms, Mandal, Babri, the nuclear tests, demonetisation, the COVID lockdown), because honest people still disagree about them.

About the numbers

Indian statistics have changed method several times (for example, the way GDP is measured was changed in 2015, and the way poverty is counted has changed at least four times). Numbers here are rounded and, where methods differ, the report says which measure it is using. The full source list is at the end.

Money and the economy

GDP (Gross Domestic Product)
The total value of everything a country produces in a year: all the food, cars, haircuts, software, bricks and bus rides added up. It is the country's "yearly income". "Nominal" GDP is counted in today's prices; "real" GDP removes the effect of rising prices so that you can compare years honestly. "PPP" (purchasing power parity) adjusts for the fact that a rupee buys more in India than a dollar buys in America.
Growth rate
How much real GDP went up compared with last year, in percent. At 3.5% a year, an economy doubles in about 20 years; at 7%, in about 10 years. India grew around 3.5% a year from 1950 to 1980 (economists mockingly called it the "Hindu rate of growth"), 5.5% in the 1980s, and 6 to 8% in most years since the 1990s.
Per capita
"Per person". GDP per capita is the national income divided by the population. It matters because a country can get richer while its people stay poor, if the population grows just as fast.
Inflation
Prices rising over time, so each rupee buys less. Measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI, the prices ordinary families pay) or the Wholesale Price Index (WPI, prices in bulk markets). High inflation hurts the poor most because their wages lag behind prices. India's worst inflation years were 1973-74 (over 20%), 1979-81, 1991 (about 14%) and 2009-13 (about 10% a year).
Budget, fiscal deficit and revenue deficit
The budget is the government's yearly plan of what it will earn (taxes) and spend. A fiscal deficit means the government spends more than it earns and borrows the difference, usually written as a percent of GDP. A small deficit is normal; a large one for years means heavy debt and higher inflation. India's central deficit was about 8% of GDP in the crisis year 1991, 9.2% in the COVID year 2020-21, and is targeted at 4.3% for 2026-27.
Public debt
Everything the government has borrowed and still owes, added up. India's central and state debt together is about 80% of GDP. The 2026 budget set a goal of bringing it down to about 50% by 2031.
Foreign exchange (forex) reserves
The country's savings in foreign money (mostly dollars) and gold, held by the Reserve Bank. They are used to pay for imports like oil when exports and foreign investment are not enough. In June 1991 India had barely two weeks' worth of imports left; in 2024 it had about ten months' worth (over $700 billion).
Balance of payments (BoP) and current account deficit
The country's bank statement with the rest of the world. If India imports more than it exports and the gap is not filled by money coming in (investment, loans, remittances from Indians abroad), reserves fall. When they fall near zero, that is a "balance of payments crisis": the country cannot pay for essential imports. India had such crises in 1957-58, 1966, 1980-81 and, worst of all, 1991.
Devaluation and the exchange rate
The exchange rate is the price of the rupee in dollars. Devaluation is the government deliberately making the rupee cheaper, which makes exports cheaper for foreigners and imports costlier for Indians. In 1947 one dollar cost about Rs 3.3; in 1966 the rupee was devalued to Rs 7.5; in 1991 to about Rs 25; the market now sets the rate, around Rs 95-96 in October 2026.
Tariff, trade deficit, exports and imports
A tariff is a tax on imported goods. High tariffs protect local factories from foreign competition but make goods costlier and local firms lazy. India's tariffs were among the world's highest (over 100% on many goods) before 1991 and are now around 10-15% on average. A trade deficit means importing more than exporting.
FDI and FII (foreign investment)
Foreign Direct Investment is when a foreign company builds or buys a business in India (a factory, a shop chain). FII or FPI (portfolio investment) is foreign money buying Indian shares and bonds, which can leave quickly. India was closed to most FDI before 1991 and now receives $70-80 billion a year.
RBI, monetary policy and the repo rate
The Reserve Bank of India is the country's central bank: it prints money, supervises banks and sets interest rates. The repo rate is the interest rate at which banks borrow from the RBI; raising it slows borrowing and cools inflation, cutting it does the opposite. Since 2016 a committee at the RBI is legally required to keep inflation around 4%.
Bad loans (NPAs) and the "twin balance sheet" problem
A non-performing asset is a loan that is not being repaid. When many big companies cannot repay, banks become weak and stop lending, which slows the whole economy. India's bad-loan pile peaked at over 11% of all loans in 2018 and was cut to about 2.5% by 2025 through a new bankruptcy law and government money put into banks.
Subsidy
Money the government pays so that something costs people less than it really costs: cheap fertiliser, cheap grain, cheap cooking gas, cheap electricity for farmers. Subsidies help the poor but are expensive and often leak to people who do not need them.
MSP, procurement and PDS (ration shops)
The Minimum Support Price is a guaranteed price at which the government promises to buy certain crops (mainly rice and wheat) from farmers. The grain is stored by the Food Corporation of India and sold cheaply or given free through the Public Distribution System, the "ration shop" network that today covers over 800 million people.
Poverty line
An income or spending level below which a person is counted as poor. India has used several lines (Lakdawala 1993, Tendulkar 2009, Rangarajan 2014) and the World Bank uses dollar-a-day lines adjusted for prices. Different lines give different numbers, so the report always says which one it is using. By every measure, poverty has fallen sharply since the 1970s, and fastest after 2005.
HDI (Human Development Index)
A United Nations score from 0 to 1 that combines income, education and life expectancy. India scored about 0.43 in 1990 and about 0.69 in 2023, ranked 130th of 193 countries: "medium human development".
Informal sector
Work that is not registered or protected: street vendors, daily-wage labourers, small shops, domestic workers, most farm work. About 85-90% of Indian workers are informal, which is why shocks like demonetisation or the COVID lockdown hurt so many people who do not show up in company statistics.
Unemployment rate and labour force participation
The unemployment rate is the share of people who want work but cannot find any. Labour force participation is the share of adults who are working or looking for work. India's puzzle is low participation among women (about 42% in 2023-24, up from 23% in 2017-18, but much of it unpaid family work) and high unemployment among educated youth (about 16% in mid-2026).
Socialism, capitalism and the mixed economy
In capitalism, private people own businesses and compete for profit. In socialism, the state owns or directs the big industries to serve a plan. Nehru chose a "mixed economy": the state runs steel, power, railways, banks and heavy industry, and private firms run the rest under strict licences. The word "socialist" was added to the Constitution's preamble in 1976.
Licence Raj (industrial licensing)
From 1951 to 1991 a factory needed government permission to start, to expand, to import a machine, or even to change what it made. The idea was to steer scarce resources to planned uses; the result was shortages, waiting lists (ten years for a telephone, years for a scooter), bribery, and firms that never had to improve. The nickname mocks the "British Raj".
Nationalisation
The government taking over private companies. Indira Gandhi nationalised 14 big banks in 1969 (six more in 1980), coal mines in 1971-73 and general insurance in 1972. Supporters say it took banks to villages; critics say it made banks political and inefficient.
Public sector (PSUs) and private sector
Public sector undertakings are companies owned by the government, like ONGC, SAIL, BHEL, Coal India, LIC, the State Bank of India. The private sector is everything owned by individuals and shareholders, like Tata, Reliance or a corner shop.
Five-Year Plans and the Planning Commission
From 1951 to 2017 a Planning Commission in Delhi wrote five-year plans saying where the country's money should go (dams, steel, schools). The Modi government replaced it in 2015 with NITI Aayog, an advisory "think tank" with no power over money.
Liberalisation, privatisation, globalisation
The three words used for the 1991 reforms: liberalisation means removing licences and controls; privatisation means selling government companies; globalisation means opening up to foreign trade and investment.
Disinvestment
The government selling some or all of its shares in a public company. "Strategic sale" means selling control to a private buyer (as with VSNL and BALCO in 2001-02, or Air India in 2021-22).
GST (Goods and Services Tax)
One nationwide tax on goods and services introduced on 1 July 2017, replacing a maze of central and state taxes that made India "one country, 30 markets". Rates are set jointly by the centre and the states in a GST Council. Simplified to two main rates (5% and 18%) in September 2025.
Demonetisation
Declaring certain currency notes worthless. Done in 1946, in 1978 (very high notes, little effect) and most famously on 8 November 2016, when Rs 500 and Rs 1,000 notes, 86% of all cash, were cancelled overnight.
IMF and World Bank
Two international lenders set up in 1944. The International Monetary Fund lends to countries that run out of foreign money, usually with conditions (cut spending, open the economy). The World Bank lends for long-term projects like dams, roads and schools. India borrowed from the IMF in 1966, 1981 and 1991.
Green Revolution
The jump in food production from the late 1960s using new high-yield wheat and rice seeds, chemical fertiliser, irrigation and guaranteed prices. It ended India's dependence on foreign food aid but also concentrated gains in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh and strained groundwater.
Demographic dividend
The economic boost a country can get when most of its people are of working age and have few dependants. India's "window" is roughly 2005-2045. It only pays off if those young people get education and jobs; otherwise it becomes a "demographic burden".
Aadhaar, JAM and DBT
Aadhaar is the 12-digit biometric identity number (started 2009, now held by over 1.4 billion people). "JAM" means linking Jan Dhan bank accounts, Aadhaar and Mobile phones. Direct Benefit Transfer means sending subsidies and pensions straight into bank accounts instead of through middlemen.

How the government works

The Constitution
India's rulebook, written between 1946 and 1949 by a Constituent Assembly, with B.R. Ambedkar heading the drafting committee, and in force since 26 January 1950. It is the longest written constitution in the world. It lists the rights of citizens, the powers of the centre and the states, how elections, courts and Parliament work, and how it can be changed.
Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles
Fundamental Rights are promises the state cannot break: equality, free speech, freedom of religion, protection of life and liberty, no untouchability. Directive Principles are goals the state should pursue (a decent living, free education, a uniform civil code) but which courts cannot enforce.
Constitutional amendment
A change to the Constitution, needing a two-thirds vote in both houses of Parliament (and for some changes, half the state legislatures). There have been 106 amendments as of 2026; the 1st (1951), 42nd (1976), 44th (1978), 73rd and 74th (1992), 101st (GST, 2016) and 106th (women's reservation, 2023) are the ones this report mentions most.
Basic structure doctrine
A rule made by the Supreme Court in 1973 (Kesavananda Bharati case): Parliament can amend the Constitution but cannot destroy its basic features such as democracy, secularism, federalism, judicial review and free elections. It is India's main brake against a government using a big majority to make itself permanent.
Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha
Parliament has two houses. The Lok Sabha (543 elected members) is chosen directly by voters every five years; whoever controls a majority there forms the government and chooses the prime minister. The Rajya Sabha (up to 250 members) is elected by state legislatures and represents the states; it can delay but not block money bills.
Majority, minority and coalition governments
A majority government is one party holding more than half the Lok Sabha seats (272 or more), so it can pass laws alone. A coalition is several parties ruling together. A minority government does not have a majority of its own and survives on "outside support" from parties that are not in the cabinet; such governments can fall any time the supporters withdraw, as happened in 1979, 1990, 1991, 1997 and 1999.
Confidence vote and no-confidence motion
A vote in the Lok Sabha on whether the government still has the support of a majority. If it loses, it must resign. Vajpayee lost one by a single vote in 1999; Manmohan Singh won one in 2008 over the nuclear deal.
President and Governors
The President is the ceremonial head of state, elected by MPs and state legislators, who acts on the advice of the cabinet but can send bills back once and holds some emergency powers. Governors are the President's representatives in each state; because they are appointed by the centre, their role in dismissing state governments or delaying bills has been fought over for decades.
President's Rule (Article 356)
The centre taking over a state's government when it believes the state cannot be run according to the Constitution. It was used over 100 times, often to remove opposition governments, until the Supreme Court's Bommai judgment (1994) made it reviewable by courts. Since then it is used much less.
Emergency (Article 352)
A declaration that suspends normal rights so the government can deal with war or rebellion. Declared during the wars of 1962 and 1971, and most notoriously on 25 June 1975 for "internal disturbance", when Indira Gandhi jailed opponents and censored the press for 21 months. The 1978 amendment made it much harder to declare.
Federalism (centre and states)
Power shared between the national government and the states. India is "quasi-federal": the centre is stronger than in the United States (it collects most taxes and can take over states), but states run police, health, schools, agriculture and land. Fights over money, language, Governors and the drawing of constituencies are a permanent feature.
Supreme Court, High Courts, PIL and the collegium
The Supreme Court is the top court and the final interpreter of the Constitution. Public Interest Litigation (PIL), invented by judges in the 1980s, lets anyone ask the court to fix a public wrong, which made courts powerful in everything from pollution to prison conditions. The "collegium" is the group of senior judges who choose new judges, a system the court created in 1993 and defended in 2015 against the government's attempt to replace it. Courts are badly overloaded: about 50 million cases are pending.
Election Commission, EVMs
The independent body that runs elections. It made India's giant elections credible (the 1952 election was the largest ever held anywhere) and under T.N. Seshan in the 1990s tamed vote-rigging and spending. Electronic voting machines replaced paper from 1999-2004. Its independence became a live dispute again in 2023-26 over how commissioners are appointed and over voter-list revisions.
Anti-defection law
A 1985 constitutional change that disqualifies legislators who switch parties after being elected. It was meant to stop the "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" buying and selling of MLAs; critics say it also turned MPs into people who cannot vote against their party leader.
Ordinance
A temporary law issued by the President (really the cabinet) when Parliament is not sitting. It lapses unless Parliament approves it within six weeks of meeting. Governments of all parties have used ordinances to avoid debate.
Delimitation
Redrawing constituency boundaries, and deciding how many Lok Sabha seats each state gets, based on population. Seat shares were frozen in 1976 (and again in 2001) so states that controlled population growth would not lose seats. The freeze ends after the first census after 2026, which is why southern states fear losing power to the populous north.

Society, religion and identity

Caste, Scheduled Castes (SC), Scheduled Tribes (ST), OBCs
Caste is the hereditary social ranking of Hindu (and, in practice, much of Indian) society. Scheduled Castes are the groups once treated as "untouchable" (about 17% of the population); Scheduled Tribes are indigenous tribal communities (about 9%); Other Backward Classes are socially and educationally disadvantaged castes above these (estimated 40-50%; the 2027 census will count them for the first time since 1931).
Reservation
Quotas in government jobs, colleges and legislatures for disadvantaged groups: 15% for SCs and 7.5% for STs since 1950, 27% for OBCs in central jobs since 1990 and in central colleges since 2006, 10% for the "economically weaker" among the rest since 2019, and one-third for women in village and city councils since 1993 (and, once seats are redrawn, in Parliament).
Mandal Commission
A 1979-80 commission that recommended 27% reservation for OBCs in central government jobs. V.P. Singh's decision to implement it in August 1990 triggered violent upper-caste protests and permanently changed Indian politics by making OBC parties powerful.
Secularism
In India's sense, the state treating all religions equally rather than keeping religion out of public life entirely. The state can regulate temples, fund pilgrim facilities and run separate personal laws for Hindus, Muslims, Christians and Parsis. What "equal treatment" means in practice is the central argument of Indian politics.
Communalism and communal riots
"Communal" in India means relating to religious community conflict, mostly Hindu-Muslim but also Hindu-Sikh (1984) and Hindu-Christian (Odisha 2008). A communal riot is violence between religious groups; the worst since 1947 were Partition (1947), Nellie (1983), Delhi (1984), Bhagalpur (1989), Mumbai (1992-93), Gujarat (2002), Muzaffarnagar (2013) and Delhi (2020).
Hindutva and Hindu nationalism
The idea, set out by V.D. Savarkar in 1923, that India is essentially a Hindu nation and that Indian identity flows from Hindu culture. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS, founded 1925) organises around it, and the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP, founded 1980 from the older Jana Sangh of 1951) is its political wing. Supporters call it cultural pride and the correction of "appeasement"; critics call it majoritarianism that makes minorities second-class.
Uniform Civil Code (UCC)
A single law of marriage, divorce, inheritance and adoption for all citizens, replacing the separate religious "personal laws". It is a Directive Principle (Article 44). Hindu personal law was reformed by Parliament in 1955-56; Muslim personal law largely was not. Uttarakhand became the first state to enact a UCC in 2025.
Article 370
The constitutional clause (1949) that gave Jammu and Kashmir its own constitution and limited the central laws that applied there, part of the terms on which the state joined India. It was hollowed out over decades and removed on 5 August 2019, when the state was split into two union territories.
AFSPA
The Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act of 1958 gives soldiers in "disturbed areas" (parts of the Northeast and Kashmir) powers to search, arrest and shoot with legal immunity. Supporters say it is needed against insurgents; critics point to killings and abuses that are rarely prosecuted.
Insurgency, terrorism, Naxalism
An insurgency is an armed rebellion against the state by people who want separation or a different system (Nagas since the 1950s, Mizos in the 1960s, Punjab in the 1980s, Kashmir since 1989). Terrorism is violence against civilians to spread fear, often by groups based in Pakistan (Mumbai 1993 and 2008, Parliament 2001, Pulwama 2019, Pahalgam 2025). Naxalism or Maoism is a communist armed movement among the poorest tribal districts of central India, which began at Naxalbari in 1967 and peaked around 2010.
Partition
The division of British India in August 1947 into India and Pakistan (East Pakistan became Bangladesh in 1971), drawn along religious lines. About 14 million people crossed the new borders and somewhere between 200,000 and two million were killed in the violence. It shaped everything that followed: the Kashmir dispute, the size of the army, Hindu-Muslim relations, and the refugee populations of Punjab, Delhi and Bengal.
Princely states
The 565 kingdoms, from Hyderabad (larger than Britain) to tiny estates, that the British ruled indirectly through local rulers. In 1947 each could join India or Pakistan. Sardar Patel and his officer V.P. Menon persuaded or pressured almost all into India within two years; Junagadh, Hyderabad and Kashmir needed force or war.

India and the world

The Cold War
The 1947-1991 global rivalry between the United States (with its allies) and the Soviet Union (with its allies). Almost every new country was pressured to pick a side. India's wars, aid, arms and economics were shaped by it: the United States armed Pakistan from 1954, the Soviet Union armed India from the 1960s.
Non-Aligned Movement (NAM)
The group of newly independent countries, founded in 1961 by Nehru, Tito (Yugoslavia), Nasser (Egypt), Sukarno (Indonesia) and Nkrumah (Ghana), that refused to join either Cold War bloc. In practice India tilted towards the Soviet Union after 1971. Since 1991 India calls its approach "strategic autonomy" or "multi-alignment": friends with everyone, allied to no one.
Nuclear deterrence, NPT, No First Use
Deterrence is the idea that having nuclear weapons stops enemies from attacking because they fear retaliation. The Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968) lets only five countries keep nuclear weapons; India refused to sign it as unfair. India tested a "peaceful" device in 1974 and weapons in 1998, and promises No First Use: it will only use nuclear weapons if attacked with them first.

India in 1947: the starting point

To judge what the governments did, you first need to see the country they started with. In August 1947 India was free, enormous, proud, and in terrible shape.

The long shadow of colonial rule

Around the year 1700, before British rule, the Indian subcontinent produced roughly a quarter of everything made in the world, about the same as China. By 1950 its share had fallen to about 4%. Economic historians (most famously Angus Maddison, whose long-run data this report uses) estimate that the average Indian's income barely grew at all in the last fifty years of British rule: about 0.2% a year, while Britain, Japan and the United States were growing many times faster. India exported raw cotton, jute, tea and opium and imported finished cloth and machines. Famines killed tens of millions: the 1876-78 and 1896-1900 famines each killed several million people, and the Bengal famine of 1943, during the Second World War, killed around three million while grain was being exported and hoarded.

British rule also left things that independent India used: about 54,000 km of railway (built mainly to move troops and raw materials, but still the largest network in Asia), ports, the telegraph, a trained civil service, courts and a common legal system, the English language, a few universities (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, all 1857), a professional army, and a small modern industrial base in textiles (Bombay, Ahmedabad), jute (Calcutta) and steel (Tata's Jamshedpur plant, 1907). It also left habits that proved hard to shake: a government that saw its job as controlling people rather than serving them, and a deep gap between the English-speaking elite and everyone else.

How freedom came

  • 1857 The great uprising against the East India Company; afterwards the British Crown rules India directly.
  • 1885 The Indian National Congress is founded. For 30 years it politely asks for a greater Indian say in government.
  • 1915-1922 Mohandas Gandhi returns from South Africa, turns Congress into a mass movement, and launches Non-Cooperation. He invents a new weapon: peaceful, disciplined, mass defiance of unjust laws.
  • 1930-34 The Salt March and Civil Disobedience. Hundreds of thousands are jailed. Ambedkar wins separate political recognition for the "Depressed Classes"; Gandhi's fast leads to the Poona Pact, which creates reserved seats instead.
  • 1940 The Muslim League, led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, demands a separate Muslim homeland: Pakistan.
  • 1942 Quit India. The whole Congress leadership is jailed for three years. Meanwhile the League grows, and Subhas Chandra Bose raises an army with Japanese help.
  • 1946 Elections show the League has won most Muslim votes. The Cabinet Mission's plan for a united, loosely federal India fails. On 16 August, "Direct Action Day" in Calcutta kills around 4,000 people in four days and sets off a chain of massacres in Bengal, Bihar and Punjab.
  • 3 June 1947 Mountbatten announces that India will be divided and freed within ten weeks. The border is drawn by a British lawyer, Cyril Radcliffe, who had never visited India, and is published two days after independence.
  • 14-15 August 1947 Pakistan and then India become free. Nehru speaks of a "tryst with destiny". Gandhi is not in Delhi: he is in Calcutta, fasting to stop the killing.

Partition: the wound the country was born with

The Partition of 1947 is the single largest forced migration in human history. Roughly 14 million people crossed the new borders in Punjab and Bengal. Estimates of the dead range from 200,000 to two million; most historians use a figure around one million. Tens of thousands of women were abducted and raped. Trains arrived full of corpses. Delhi's population jumped by a third with refugees; Calcutta absorbed millions from East Bengal over the following decades.

Partition also divided the economy. West Punjab's wheat and canal-irrigated farms went to Pakistan, so India, which kept most of the people, lost grain. East Bengal grew the jute but the jute mills were in Calcutta, so India had mills without raw material and Pakistan had raw material without mills. The army, the treasury (India had to transfer Rs 55 crore to Pakistan, a bitterly contested payment Gandhi fasted to secure), the railways and the civil service were all split.

And it created the Kashmir problem. The princely state of Jammu and Kashmir had a Hindu ruler and a Muslim majority. In October 1947 Pakistan-backed tribal fighters invaded; the Maharaja signed the accession to India on 26 October; Indian troops were flown in the next morning. The first India-Pakistan war ran until a ceasefire on 1 January 1949, leaving the state divided along a line that is still the de facto border.

The country, by the numbers

India around 1947-1951 (figures from the 1951 Census, the first national income estimates, and later official back-calculations)

The six big founding choices

Between 1947 and 1950 the new leaders made choices that every later government inherited. Some were brave, some were argued over then and are still argued over now.

  1. Every adult votes. Universal adult franchise from day one, in a country where more than four in five people could not read. Britain itself had only given all adults the vote in 1928, the United States only fully in 1965. Many foreign observers expected the experiment to fail.
  2. A secular state, not a Hindu one. After a Partition made in the name of religion, a Hindu state was the obvious option. Nehru, Patel, Azad and Ambedkar chose instead a state with no official religion and equal citizenship for the 35 million Muslims who had stayed. Hindu nationalists have contested this choice ever since.
  3. A union with a strong centre. India is federal, but the centre keeps the big taxes and can take over states. This was chosen deliberately after watching the country nearly fall apart in 1947.
  4. Hindi and English together. Hindi became the official language, but English was kept for 15 years (and, in practice, forever) because the south refused to be ruled in a language it did not speak.
  5. A mixed economy run by plans. Inspired by the Soviet Union's rapid industrialisation and by the Depression's discredit of free markets, the state would own the "commanding heights" (steel, power, railways, heavy industry) and license the private sector.
  6. Friends with everyone, allied to no one. Non-alignment in the Cold War, and a hope that moral example and the United Nations could replace armies. The 1962 war with China would test this badly.

The Constitution of 1950

The Constituent Assembly (299 members, elected indirectly in 1946) met for nearly three years. B.R. Ambedkar, a Dalit lawyer who had spent his life fighting caste, chaired the drafting committee. The Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950, the date chosen because Congress had declared "complete independence" on that day in 1930.

What it did, in plain terms: it guaranteed Fundamental Rights to every citizen; abolished untouchability (Article 17); created a Parliament with two houses and a Supreme Court; divided powers between the centre and the states; reserved seats for Scheduled Castes and Tribes; set out Directive Principles such as free education and a uniform civil code as goals; and gave the centre emergency powers that Ambedkar warned could be abused.

"However good a Constitution may be, if those who are implementing it are not good, it will prove to be bad... In politics we will have equality and in social and economic life we will have inequality."B.R. Ambedkar's last speech to the Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949. He also warned against "hero-worship" in politics as "a sure road to degradation and to eventual dictatorship".

With that warning on the record, the governments began.

Jawaharlal Nehru: laying the foundations

The longest-serving prime minister (16 years, 9 months), Nehru took a starving, divided, barely literate country and gave it a democracy, a constitution, a planned economy, a scientific establishment and a voice in the world. He also left behind the Kashmir dispute, a humiliating defeat by China, and an economic model that would take forty years to unwind.

What he inherited

Everything described in the previous section: a country of 340 million people, 18% of whom could read, with a life expectancy of about 32 years, a food deficit, almost no industry, a war in Kashmir, a million dead from Partition, millions of refugees, 565 princely states not yet part of India, no constitution, and a Cold War starting outside. Within six months Gandhi was assassinated (30 January 1948) by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist who blamed him for being soft on Muslims and Pakistan. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) was banned for 17 months.

The conditions he worked under

  • No money and no engineers. India had a handful of trained scientists and engineers, a tiny tax base, and almost no savings to invest. Foreign aid was small until the late 1950s.
  • The monsoon ran the economy. With little irrigation, a bad monsoon meant hunger and a shrinking economy (as in 1957-58).
  • The Cold War closed in. From 1954 the United States armed Pakistan as an ally against communism (through the SEATO and CENTO pacts). This pushed India towards the Soviet Union for arms and heavy industry, while Nehru tried to stay neutral.
  • China changed. The communist victory of 1949 and China's occupation of Tibet in 1950 put a powerful, ambitious state on India's longest and least-defined border.
  • Few examples to copy. Most of Asia and Africa was still colonised. The models on offer were Soviet planning (which looked spectacularly successful then) and Western capitalism (which had just produced the Great Depression).

What the government did

Politics and nation-building

Holding the country together. Patel and his civil servant V.P. Menon persuaded, pressured or bought out the rulers of 565 princely states with pensions ("privy purses") and titles. Three resisted: Junagadh (taken over in November 1947, confirmed by a vote), Hyderabad, the largest, whose Nizam wanted independence and whose Razakar militia terrorised Hindus (taken in a five-day army action, "Operation Polo", in September 1948, followed by reprisal killings of Muslims that a government report put at 27,000 to 40,000), and Kashmir (the war, the ceasefire, and the special status of Article 370). Goa was taken from Portugal by the army in December 1961 after years of failed talks. Pondicherry was handed over by France in 1954.

Elections that worked. The first general election (1951-52) required 224,000 polling booths, 2 million steel ballot boxes and symbols on ballots so that illiterate voters could choose. Chief Election Commissioner Sukumar Sen ran it so well that the model was copied across the newly free world. Nehru campaigned hard but also encouraged a real opposition; he once wrote an anonymous article warning the country against his own possible dictatorial tendencies.

Redrawing the map by language. In December 1952 Potti Sriramulu fasted to death for a Telugu-speaking state. Riots followed, Andhra was carved out of Madras in 1953, and the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 redrew India into states based on language. Nehru had feared this would break the country up. Instead it probably saved it: once people could be governed in their own language, the separatist steam went out of most language movements. (Bombay was split into Maharashtra and Gujarat in 1960; Punjabi-speaking Punjab came in 1966; Nagaland was created in 1963 to settle a bloody insurgency.)

Hindi and English. The Constitution said Hindi would replace English in 15 years, in 1965. The south, especially Tamil Nadu, refused. Nehru promised in 1959 that English would stay as long as non-Hindi states wanted it, and the Official Languages Act of 1963 wrote that into law.

The dark spots. The First Amendment (1951) allowed the government to restrict free speech on grounds of "public order" and protected land reform laws from courts, partly in response to court rulings against the government. The Preventive Detention Act (1950) allowed jailing without trial. In 1959 Nehru dismissed Kerala's elected communist government (the first elected communist government anywhere in the world) using President's Rule, after an agitation by the church, caste groups and his own Congress, pushed by his daughter Indira, then Congress president. It set a precedent that was abused for 35 years.

The economy: planning the "commanding heights"

In simple words

Nehru's economic idea was this: India is poor because it has no modern industry. Private businessmen are too few and too small to build steel plants and dams. So the state must build them, using five-year plans, while keeping private firms on a short leash of licences so they serve the plan. Foreign goods would be kept out with high tariffs so Indian factories could grow ("import substitution"). The model is called the mixed economy.

  • First Plan (1951-56): mostly agriculture, irrigation and power. Giant dams began: Bhakra-Nangal (Punjab), Hirakud (Odisha), Damodar Valley (Bengal-Bihar), Nagarjuna Sagar (Andhra). Nehru called them "the temples of modern India". Growth beat the target: 3.6% a year.
  • Second Plan (1956-61): the Mahalanobis model, heavy industry first. Three public steel plants were built with foreign help: Bhilai (Soviet), Rourkela (West German), Durgapur (British). State companies were born that still exist: ONGC (oil, 1956), Indian Oil (1959), Hindustan Machine Tools, Bharat Heavy Electricals, Hindustan Antibiotics. The Imperial Bank became the State Bank of India (1955); 245 private insurers were merged into the Life Insurance Corporation (1956); Air India was nationalised (1953). The 1956 Industrial Policy Resolution reserved 17 industries for the state.
  • The Licence Raj was born: the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951 required permission for almost any industrial activity. When India ran short of dollars in 1957-58, import controls were tightened further. This was not meant to last forever; it lasted until 1991.
  • Land reform: the zamindari system, where big landlords collected rent from tenants, was abolished by state laws by the mid-1950s, giving around 20 million tenants ownership. But ceilings on land holdings were widely evaded, and the 1959 Nagpur resolution for cooperative farming was abandoned after a backlash led by Charan Singh.
  • Results: the economy grew about 3.8% a year on average from 1951 to 1964, three to four times faster than under the British, and income per person grew about 1.7% a year. Industry grew about 7% a year. Foodgrain output rose from 51 million tonnes (1950-51) to 82 million (1960-61). Savings rose from 10% of GDP to about 14%. Prices were stable for most of the 1950s. But by 1963-64 the Third Plan was off track, food was short again, prices were rising, and foreign exchange was scarce.
  • Scandals: the "jeep scandal" (1948, Krishna Menon's purchase of army jeeps in London) and the Mundhra affair (1957-58, when LIC was made to buy shares in a dubious businessman's firms; Finance Minister T.T. Krishnamachari resigned after an inquiry led by a young lawyer, M.C. Chagla, held in public).

Society and social reform

  • The Hindu Code Bills (1955-56) were the biggest social reform of the era: the Hindu Marriage Act banned polygamy and allowed divorce; the Hindu Succession Act gave daughters a share of inheritance; adoption and guardianship laws were modernised. Conservative Hindus, including President Rajendra Prasad, fought them bitterly; Ambedkar resigned as Law Minister in 1951 when Nehru delayed. Nehru pushed them through after the 1952 election. Muslim personal law was left untouched, a choice that has shaped the Uniform Civil Code argument ever since.
  • Caste: untouchability was made a crime in 1955. Reservations for Scheduled Castes and Tribes in jobs, colleges and legislatures began. In practice, caste discrimination continued almost everywhere, especially in villages. Ambedkar converted to Buddhism with half a million followers in 1956, two months before his death.
  • Health: the National Malaria Control Programme (1953) cut cases from an estimated 75 million a year to about 100,000 by the mid-1960s, one of the great public-health successes of the century. Life expectancy rose from about 32 to about 45 by 1964. The All India Institute of Medical Sciences opened in 1956.
  • Population: India launched the world's first national family planning programme in 1952, but it was tiny and population growth accelerated from 1.25% to 2% a year as deaths fell faster than births.
  • Education: the big miss. Nehru built the top: the five IITs (Kharagpur 1951, Bombay, Madras, Kanpur, Delhi by 1961), the first IIMs (1961), the University Grants Commission (1956), a chain of national laboratories under the CSIR. But primary education was starved. Literacy crawled from 18% (1951) to 28% (1961), while South Korea, Taiwan and China were pushing close to universal primary schooling. Economists like Amartya Sen later argued this was the single most costly mistake of the era.
  • Refugees: about 7-8 million refugees were resettled, successfully in Punjab and Delhi (where refugee colonies became middle-class suburbs), much less so in Bengal, where migration from East Pakistan continued for decades.
  • Tribal and Northeast policy: Nehru's "tribal panchsheel" said tribes should develop "along the lines of their own genius", and the Sixth Schedule gave autonomy to hill areas. But the Naga demand for independence turned into an armed insurgency; the army went in (1956) and the AFSPA was passed in 1958. It is still in force in parts of the Northeast.

Religion and secularism

Nehru's secularism meant the state kept its distance from religion and protected minorities. Examples: he objected to President Prasad attending the 1951 reopening of the Somnath temple in an official capacity; he signed the Nehru-Liaquat Pact (1950) with Pakistan to protect minorities on both sides after riots in East Bengal; he kept Muslims like Azad in the top ranks of the state. Hindu nationalists formed the Bharatiya Jana Sangh in 1951 (its founder, Syama Prasad Mookerjee, died in Kashmir custody in 1953 after protesting the state's special status). Cow slaughter was left to the states, and many banned it in the 1950s. Communal violence was low through the 1950s, partly from exhaustion after Partition. Among Sikhs, a demand for a Punjabi-speaking state (the "Punjabi Suba") grew under Master Tara Singh; Nehru refused it, fearing a religious state; it was granted by his daughter in 1966.

Foreign policy and security

  • Non-alignment: India refused to join either Cold War bloc, helped end the Korean War (an Indian general chaired the prisoner-repatriation commission, 1953), chaired the ceasefire commission in Indochina (1954), sent large peacekeeping forces to Congo (1960-64), and co-founded the Non-Aligned Movement (1961). India stayed in the Commonwealth as a republic, a formula Nehru invented. Critics noted that he condemned Britain and France over Suez (1956) loudly but the Soviet invasion of Hungary (1956) quietly.
  • Pakistan: after the Kashmir war, Nehru took the dispute to the United Nations in January 1948, expecting the UN to brand Pakistan the aggressor. Instead the UN called for a plebiscite (vote) that never happened, and the issue became permanent. The one lasting success was the Indus Waters Treaty (1960), brokered by the World Bank, which shared the six rivers of Punjab and survived three wars (India put it "in abeyance" in April 2025).
  • China and the 1962 war: Nehru accepted Chinese control of Tibet (1950) and signed the Panchsheel agreement on peaceful coexistence (1954), with the slogan "Hindi-Chini bhai-bhai". But China had quietly built a road through Aksai Chin (discovered by India in 1957-58), and in 1959 India gave asylum to the Dalai Lama. Border clashes began. India's "forward policy" of placing small posts in disputed areas, with an army starved of equipment under Defence Minister Krishna Menon, invited a response. On 20 October 1962 China attacked in Ladakh and Arunachal (then NEFA), routed the Indian army, and declared a unilateral ceasefire on 21 November, keeping Aksai Chin. About 1,400 Indian soldiers were killed and nearly 4,000 captured. Nehru had asked the United States for air support. Menon resigned; defence spending doubled; the army grew from 550,000 to 825,000; the non-aligned dream of moral power without military power was dead. Nehru never recovered.
  • Nuclear and space: the Atomic Energy Commission was set up in 1948 under Homi Bhabha, India's first reactor (Apsara) ran in 1956, and the Canadian-supplied CIRUS reactor (1960) would later produce the plutonium for the 1974 test. Nehru opposed the bomb publicly but refused to close the option. The space programme began in 1962 under Vikram Sarabhai; the first sounding rocket flew from Thumba in Kerala in November 1963, carried to the launch pad on a bicycle.

Culture and science

The Nehru years were the golden age of Hindi cinema (Raj Kapoor's Awaara was a hit even in the Soviet Union; Guru Dutt; Mehboob Khan's Mother India, 1957, was nominated for an Oscar) and the arrival of world-class Indian art cinema (Satyajit Ray's Pather Panchali, 1955). The state built the cultural institutions: the Sahitya, Sangeet Natak and Lalit Kala Akademis (1953-54), the National School of Drama, the Film and Television Institute, the national film awards. Doordarshan began experimental television in Delhi in 1959. Le Corbusier designed Chandigarh. Nehru preached "scientific temper" and spent generously on laboratories. In sport, India won Olympic hockey gold in 1948, 1952 and 1956, hosted the first Asian Games (1951), won its first cricket Test (1952), and K.D. Jadhav won India's first individual Olympic medal (wrestling, 1952).

The ups

  • Democracy survived and took root; three free elections were held. Almost no other new nation of that era managed this (Pakistan had its first coup in 1958).
  • The country stayed united through Partition, princely integration, and the language crisis.
  • Food output went up 60%; malaria was nearly beaten; life expectancy rose by over 10 years.
  • Institutions that still carry India were built: the Constitution, Election Commission, Supreme Court, IITs, AIIMS, ISRO's and the nuclear programme's roots, the public steel and power base.
  • Women got legal rights in marriage and inheritance that no other large non-Western country had in the 1950s.
  • India earned a global voice far bigger than its power.

The downs

  • The 1962 defeat: a failure of judgment, preparation and the choice of Krishna Menon.
  • Kashmir frozen into a permanent dispute, partly by taking it to the UN.
  • Primary education and literacy neglected; population growth not addressed.
  • The Licence Raj: controls that would strangle growth for four decades, and the start of bureaucratic corruption.
  • The First Amendment and the Kerala dismissal: early precedents for curbing speech and misusing central power.
  • Land reform was half-done; rural poverty barely moved.
  • Indira Gandhi's rise as Congress president in 1959 planted the dynastic habit.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

  • Schools before steel. Japan in the 1870s, and South Korea and Taiwan in the 1950s, put mass primary education first and grew rich on an educated workforce. India built excellent universities for the few and left most children unschooled. The cost was decades of low productivity.
  • Export what you are good at. India had cheap labour and a textile tradition. An export-led path like the one Korea and Taiwan took from the 1960s might have created millions of factory jobs. Instead, high tariffs and licences protected inefficient producers.
  • Keep Kashmir out of the UN, or settle early. Several historians argue a settlement along the ceasefire line was possible in the early 1950s when India held the stronger hand.
  • Take China seriously. The warnings (from Patel in 1950, from army chiefs in 1959-61) were there. A properly equipped army and a less provocative forward policy might have avoided 1962 or changed its result.
  • Enforce land reform and family planning. Both were on paper; neither was pushed through the Congress's own landlord base.

Could have been worse

  • India could have become a military dictatorship (Pakistan, Burma, Indonesia, Egypt, Ghana and dozens of others did within 15 years). Nehru kept the army firmly under civilians and treated Parliament with respect.
  • It could have become a Hindu state after a religious Partition, with 35 million Muslims as second-class citizens. The leaders chose otherwise, at real political cost.
  • It could have broken up over language (the "Balkanisation" that every foreign expert predicted). Linguistic states, grudgingly accepted, prevented it.
  • It could have joined a Cold War bloc and been dragged into foreign wars or coups; non-alignment kept India out of them.
  • Unlike the colonial period, there was no famine with mass deaths after 1947. Democracy and a free press made famine politically impossible, as Amartya Sen later showed.

What it chose

Nehru chose democracy over speed, unity over uniformity, a state-led economy over markets, higher education over mass schooling, and moral influence over military strength. The first two choices look wiser every decade. The economic and educational choices look worse the more we learn about what East Asia did instead. The security choice failed in 1962, and every government since has over-corrected for it.

What changed after

Nehru's successors kept his frame (democracy, secularism, planning, non-alignment) for a quarter century. The food crisis of 1965-67 forced the first big change: a shift from heavy industry to the Green Revolution. The 1967 election ended Congress's automatic dominance. His daughter turned his party from a federation of state leaders into a family-run machine. The 1991 reforms dismantled his economic model, though his public-sector companies and institutes remain. Since 2014 his idea of secularism has been openly challenged. The things he built that no one has tried to undo are the ones that matter most: elections, the courts, the IITs, the scientific agencies, and the habit of arguing rather than shooting.

Lal Bahadur Shastri: the small man who faced famine and war

A quiet, honest, five-foot-two politician whom few expected to matter, Shastri led India for only nineteen months. In that time he fought a war with Pakistan to a standstill, launched the strategy that became the Green Revolution, defused a language revolt in the south, and died in a foreign city hours after signing a peace he was being attacked for at home.

What he inherited

A country still shaken by the 1962 defeat and spending heavily on rearmament; food running short after a weak harvest, with prices rising 10% or more a year; foreign exchange nearly exhausted; unrest in Kashmir after the theft of a holy relic from the Hazratbal shrine (December 1963); and a constitutional deadline of 26 January 1965 for Hindi to replace English, which the south refused to accept. In Pakistan, President Ayub Khan had concluded that India after Nehru was weak and that Kashmir was ripe for the taking.

The conditions he worked under

  • Two failed monsoons (1965 and 1966) produced the worst food shortage since 1943. The 1965-66 harvest fell from 89 to 72 million tonnes. India lived "ship to mouth" on American wheat under the PL-480 aid programme; President Johnson released shipments month by month to pressure India on policy, a humiliation Indians never forgot.
  • Pakistan was armed by America with Patton tanks and Sabre jets, and had just made a border deal with China (1963). Its generals believed they had a window before India's post-1962 rearmament was complete.
  • Money: the Third Plan was collapsing; aid donors (the World Bank-led consortium) were pressing India to devalue the rupee and loosen controls as a condition for more loans.

What the government did

Food: the turn towards the Green Revolution

In simple words

Until 1964 the plan was: build factories now, fix farming later. Shastri reversed the order. If India could not feed itself, nothing else would work. His slogan "Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan" (hail the soldier, hail the farmer) said that food and defence came first.

  • He appointed C. Subramaniam as agriculture minister and backed a "New Agricultural Strategy" (1965): import 18,000 tonnes of dwarf Mexican wheat seed developed by Norman Borlaug, push chemical fertiliser, concentrate on irrigated districts, and, crucially, guarantee prices. The Agricultural Prices Commission (which sets the MSP) and the Food Corporation of India (which buys and stores grain) were both created in January 1965. The harvest of 1967-68 (95 million tonnes, a record) would prove the strategy right, but Shastri did not live to see it.
  • He visited the Amul dairy cooperative at Anand in October 1964, was impressed, and asked Verghese Kurien to copy it nationwide. The National Dairy Development Board (1965) grew into "Operation Flood", which made India the world's largest milk producer by 1998.
  • He asked Indians to skip one meal a week so the grain could go to those who had none, and fasted himself. Rationing was extended to the cities.
  • Quietly, he began to loosen Nehru's model: the Planning Commission lost some power, the prime minister's own secretariat (the origin of today's PMO) was strengthened, and talks began with the World Bank on devaluation and fewer controls. His successor would take the blame for the devaluation he had set in motion.

The language crisis of 1965

When the Hindi deadline arrived on 26 January 1965, Tamil Nadu (then Madras State) exploded. Students burned themselves alive; police firing and riots killed over 60 people; two central ministers from the state resigned. Shastri went on radio to promise that English would continue as an official language for as long as non-Hindi states wanted. That promise was written into law in 1967 and has kept the peace on language ever since. It also made the Tamil nationalist DMK, which swept the state in 1967, a permanent power; Congress has never ruled Tamil Nadu again.

The 1965 war with Pakistan

  • April 1965 Pakistan probes Indian defences in the Rann of Kutch, a salt marsh in Gujarat. A British-brokered ceasefire sends the dispute to arbitration.
  • 5 August "Operation Gibraltar": thousands of Pakistani soldiers in disguise cross into Kashmir to start an uprising. Kashmiris do not rise; many report the infiltrators to the army.
  • 1 September Pakistan's armour attacks at Chhamb to cut the road to Kashmir.
  • 6 September Shastri's decision: rather than fight only in Kashmir, India crosses the international border towards Lahore and Sialkot. Pakistan's tank thrust at Khem Karan is destroyed at Asal Uttar; India captures the strategic Haji Pir pass.
  • Mid-September China issues an ultimatum over Sikkim; India ignores it. The United States cuts off arms and aid to both sides, which hurts Pakistan far more.
  • 23 September Ceasefire under a UN resolution. Militarily a draw (about 3,000 Indian and 3,800 Pakistani dead); politically a clear Indian win, because Pakistan's aim of seizing Kashmir failed.
  • 4-10 January 1966 Soviet Premier Kosygin hosts Shastri and Ayub in Tashkent. The Tashkent Declaration returns both armies to pre-war lines, including Haji Pir. Indian opinion is furious. Shastri dies in his room that night.

Conspiracy theories about his death (poisoning, a cover-up) have circulated for sixty years. No post-mortem was done, and his family doubted the official account, but no evidence of foul play has ever surfaced.

The ups

  • Proved that India could be led, and defended, without Nehru. The army's confidence, shattered in 1962, was restored.
  • Set the policies (price guarantees, new seeds, the FCI, the dairy board) that ended India's dependence on foreign food within a decade.
  • Settled the Hindi-English question with a promise that has held.
  • Personal honesty that became legend: he died owning almost nothing, with an unpaid loan on a small Fiat car that his widow repaid.

The downs

  • The economy shrank 3.7% in 1965-66; prices rose over 10%; the famine in Bihar in 1966-67 was only just averted.
  • Haji Pir was handed back at Tashkent, which Indians saw as giving away a victory, and the dispute with Pakistan went back to square one.
  • Dependence on American wheat, and American pressure, reached its humiliating peak.
  • The deaths in the Madras language riots.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

The new agricultural strategy could have been adopted a few years earlier (the seeds and the science existed by 1962), sparing the 1965-67 crisis. At Tashkent, a harder bargain on Haji Pir might have been possible, though with food running out and the United States cutting aid, India could not have sustained a longer war.

Could have been worse

Had India fought only inside Kashmir in September 1965, as Pakistan expected, it might have lost the state. Had the government panicked and clung to the old plan, the 1966-67 drought could have become a famine on the 1943 scale. It did not: rationing, imports and a free press kept deaths low. Had Shastri given in to Hindi hardliners in 1965, the south might have gone the way of East Pakistan.

What it chose

Food self-reliance over heavy industry, firmness over caution with Pakistan, compromise over pride on language, and peace over a victory parade at Tashkent.

Government 3 · 24 January 1966 to 24 March 1977

Indira Gandhi, first period: Green Revolution, Bangladesh, and the Emergency

Chosen as a puppet, Indira Gandhi became the most powerful prime minister India has had. In eleven years she broke the old Congress, nationalised the banks, won India's only decisive war, tested a nuclear device, ended food imports, and then suspended democracy for 21 months. Admirers remember 1971; critics remember 1975. Both are right.

Party
Indian National Congress; after the 1969 split, Congress (R), which the courts and voters recognised as the real Congress
Type of government
Majority throughout, but a shrinking one in 1967 (283 of 520 seats, and Congress lost eight states); a minority government after the 1969 split, kept alive by communist and regional support; then a huge majority after the 1971 election (352 of 518 seats).
Key people
Morarji Desai (Deputy PM and Finance until 1969), Y.B. Chavan, Jagjivan Ram, Swaran Singh, P.N. Haksar (principal secretary, the brain of 1969-73), C. Subramaniam, D.P. Defeated in the March 1977 election, which she had called herself; she lost her own seat.

What she inherited

A near-famine (the 1966-67 Bihar drought), an economy that had just shrunk, double-digit inflation, foreign exchange almost gone, an American president doling out wheat month by month, a World Bank demanding devaluation, a ceasefire but no peace with Pakistan, and a party run by old men who expected her to obey. Her father's prestige and her own name were her only assets.

The conditions she worked under

  • Two more drought years (1966-67 and 1972-73), then the 1973 oil shock, when Arab producers quadrupled the oil price: India's import bill exploded and inflation hit 20-25% in 1973-75.
  • Ten million refugees fled into India from East Pakistan in 1971, costing more than the war that followed.
  • The United States tilted to Pakistan under Nixon and Kissinger, who were using Pakistan to open relations with China. The Soviet Union became India's main arms supplier and protector.
  • Political storms: the 1967 election ended Congress dominance; the Naxalbari peasant uprising (1967) began a Maoist insurgency; the Syndicate tried to control her; after 1973 a mass movement led by Jayaprakash Narayan demanded her removal.

What the government did

Years of weakness, 1966-69

  • The 1966 devaluation. In June 1966, under pressure from the World Bank and the United States, which promised more aid in return, the rupee was devalued by 36.5% (from Rs 4.76 to Rs 7.50 per dollar) and some import controls were eased. The promised aid came late and small, prices rose, and the political backlash was so severe that she turned against economic liberalisation for the rest of her career. India would not try again until 1991.
  • The Green Revolution arrived. With Shastri's strategy in place, new seeds, fertiliser and guaranteed prices, the 1967-68 wheat harvest was a record; by 1971 foodgrain output passed 108 million tonnes, and by the mid-1970s India stopped importing food. Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh became rich farming regions. The costs came later: falling water tables, chemical overuse, and a widening gap with the dry regions that the revolution skipped.
  • Punjab and Haryana were created in November 1966, giving Sikhs the Punjabi-speaking state her father had refused. In 1966 the Mizo National Front rebelled; the air force bombed Aizawl in March 1966, the only time Indian warplanes have attacked an Indian town, and the army forcibly regrouped villages. Mizoram stayed in rebellion until 1986.
  • The cow-slaughter riot of November 1966: a mob of sadhus demanding a national ban on cow slaughter attacked Parliament; police firing killed at least eight; Home Minister Nanda resigned. She did not pass the ban.
  • The 1967 election cut Congress to 283 of 520 seats and cost it eight states, including Tamil Nadu (to the DMK, forever) and Kerala and West Bengal (to communist-led fronts). "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" defections became a national joke.

The leftward turn and the capture of Congress, 1969-71

In simple words

To beat the old party bosses, Indira went over their heads to the poor. She took the banks from private owners, cut the pensions of the former princes, and promised to "abolish poverty". The bosses expelled her; the voters backed her. The Congress stopped being a party of regional leaders and became her personal machine.

  • Bank nationalisation (19 July 1969): the 14 largest private banks, holding 85% of deposits, became government banks. Branches went from about 8,000 to over 30,000 by 1980, mostly in villages; farmers and small businesses got credit for the first time. The price: lending by political order, bad loans, and a banking system that would need repeated rescues.
  • The Congress split (November 1969): she backed V.V. Giri against the party's own candidate for President and won; the Syndicate expelled her; she took most MPs with her. Her Congress (R) governed with communist and DMK support.
  • Privy purses abolished (1971) after a first attempt was struck down by the Supreme Court. The Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act (1969) capped the size of big business houses. The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (1973) forced foreign companies to dilute ownership (IBM and Coca-Cola would leave in 1977 rather than comply). Coal (1971-73) and general insurance (1972) were nationalised; even the wholesale wheat trade was nationalised in 1973, a disaster reversed within a year.
  • "Garibi Hatao" (Abolish Poverty): the slogan of the 1971 election, held a year early. Congress (R) won 352 seats, a landslide. The old Congress (O) was wiped out.

1971: the Bangladesh war

  • December 1970 Pakistan's first free election is won by the Awami League of East Pakistan (Bengali-speaking, poorer, and a majority of Pakistan's population). West Pakistan's generals refuse to hand over power.
  • 25 March 1971 The Pakistan army launches a massacre in Dhaka ("Operation Searchlight"). Over the following months, estimates of Bengali deaths range from 300,000 to three million. Ten million refugees flee into West Bengal, Assam and Tripura. India feeds them at ruinous cost.
  • April-November India trains and arms the Mukti Bahini (Bengali guerrillas). General Manekshaw tells the prime minister the army needs time until the winter, when Himalayan passes close to China; she waits. On 9 August India signs a 20-year Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation with the Soviet Union, insurance against China and the United States.
  • 3 December Pakistan attacks Indian airbases in the west. India invades East Pakistan from three sides.
  • 16 December Pakistan's eastern army, 93,000 men, surrenders at Dhaka, the largest military surrender since the Second World War. Bangladesh is born. Nixon's "tilt" to Pakistan, including sending the aircraft carrier USS Enterprise into the Bay of Bengal, fails to change anything.
  • July 1972 The Simla Agreement with Pakistan's Zulfikar Ali Bhutto: the ceasefire line becomes the "Line of Control", and both agree to settle disputes bilaterally (India's basis ever since for rejecting outside mediation on Kashmir). India returns the prisoners and captured territory without a Kashmir settlement, a decision debated to this day.

Indira Gandhi's popularity reached a peak no Indian leader had touched; the opposition's Vajpayee reportedly compared her to the goddess Durga.

1972-75: inflation, protest, and the Supreme Court

  • The economy soured. Drought in 1972-73, the 1973 oil shock, and the cost of the refugees and war pushed inflation to over 20% in 1973-74 and 25% in 1974-75, the worst in independent India's history. Growth from 1966 to 1977 averaged only 3.6%. Poverty hit its recorded peak: about 55% of Indians were below the official line in 1973-74. A railway strike of 1.7 million workers (May 1974), led by George Fernandes, was crushed with mass arrests. Her 1974 anti-inflation measures (compulsory savings, wage freezes, credit squeeze) did bring prices down by 1975-76.
  • The Supreme Court drew a line. In the Kesavananda Bharati case (April 1973) a 13-judge bench ruled that Parliament may amend the Constitution but cannot destroy its basic structure. The next day she superseded the three most senior judges to appoint a pliant Chief Justice, A.N. Ray. The idea of a "committed judiciary" alarmed democrats.
  • The nuclear test (18 May 1974): "Smiling Buddha", a "peaceful nuclear explosion" at Pokhran in Rajasthan, made India the sixth country to test. Canada and the United States cut nuclear cooperation; the Nuclear Suppliers Group was created to stop others following India's path. India then did nothing further for 24 years.
  • Sikkim was absorbed as a state in 1975 after a referendum, following unrest against its ruler; China still calls it an annexation.
  • The JP movement. Student protests against corruption and price rises in Gujarat (the Navnirman movement) brought down the state government in 1974. In Bihar, the aged Gandhian Jayaprakash Narayan took up the cause and called for "Total Revolution" against the Congress, including appeals to the army and police to disobey "unjust" orders. Opposition parties united behind him.
  • 12 June 1975: the Allahabad High Court found her guilty of minor election malpractice in her 1971 campaign (using a government officer and facilities) and barred her from office for six years. The Supreme Court gave a partial stay. The opposition demanded she resign. JP announced a mass campaign.

The Emergency, 25 June 1975 to 21 March 1977

In simple words

An Emergency lets the government suspend citizens' rights to deal with a war or rebellion. Indira Gandhi used it to deal with her own political crisis. For 21 months India was a democracy on paper only: opponents in jail, the press censored, elections postponed, the Constitution rewritten to protect her, and her unelected son running the government's harshest programmes.

  • How it happened: late on the night of 25 June 1975, on her advice, President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed signed a proclamation of Emergency for "internal disturbance". The cabinet was told the next morning. Electricity to Delhi's newspapers was cut. By dawn, JP, Morarji Desai, Vajpayee, Advani, Charan Singh, Chandra Shekhar and hundreds of others were in jail.
  • What was done: about 110,000 people were jailed without trial under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), many for the whole period. The press was censored (the Indian Express ran a blank editorial in protest; most newspapers complied; L.K. Advani later said they "crawled" when asked to bend). The RSS and the Jamaat-e-Islami were banned. The Lok Sabha's term was extended by a year. The 42nd Amendment (1976) added "socialist" and "secular" to the Constitution's preamble, but also put the prime minister's election beyond judicial challenge, limited the courts' power of review, and extended Parliament's term. In the ADM Jabalpur case (1976) the Supreme Court ruled that during an Emergency a citizen had no right to go to court even against unlawful detention; the lone dissenter, Justice H.R. Khanna, was later passed over for Chief Justice.
  • Sanjay Gandhi's programmes: her younger son, holding no office, ran a "five-point programme" with two brutal parts. Forced sterilisation: about 8.3 million operations in 1976-77 (three times the previous year), with quotas for officials, men dragged from buses and villages, at least 1,700 deaths recorded, and police firing on protesters at Muzaffarnagar. Slum clearance: in April 1976 bulldozers demolished the homes of tens of thousands around Delhi's Turkman Gate; police fired on residents, killing at least six by the official count.
  • What supporters pointed to: inflation fell to near zero, trains ran on time, strikes stopped, officials turned up to work, and a 20-point programme promised land to the landless and relief from debt. Many middle-class Indians approved at the time. Foreign exchange reserves actually rose, helped by remittances from the first wave of Indians going to the Gulf after the oil boom.
  • The end: believing her own intelligence reports of popularity, she called an election in January 1977, released the prisoners and lifted censorship. The opposition merged into the Janata Party. In March, north India voted her out; Congress won 154 seats against Janata's 295; she and Sanjay both lost their constituencies. The Emergency was lifted on 21 March 1977.

Society, religion and culture in these years

  • Women: the "Towards Equality" report of 1974 documented how little women's lives had changed and became the founding text of the Indian women's movement. The Equal Remuneration Act (1976) and the Bonded Labour Abolition Act (1976) were passed during the Emergency. The Integrated Child Development Services (1975), today the world's largest child nutrition scheme, began.
  • Caste: the Dalit Panthers (1972) brought militant Dalit politics to Maharashtra. Across north India, the Green Revolution's new rich peasants from middle castes (Jats, Yadavs, Kurmis) began demanding power, a trend that would explode after 1990.
  • Environment: the Chipko movement (1973) of Himalayan villagers hugging trees to stop loggers; the Wildlife Protection Act (1972) and Project Tiger (1973); and her speech at the 1972 Stockholm conference arguing that "poverty is the worst polluter".
  • Religion: communal violence was relatively low in the 1960s but flared in the 1970s (Ahmedabad 1969, over 500 dead; Bhiwandi 1970). The Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973) of the Sikh Akali Dal demanded more autonomy for Punjab, a document later used by both separatists and the state to justify their actions.
  • Culture: this was the age of Amitabh Bachchan's "angry young man" (Zanjeer 1973, Deewaar 1975), of Sholay (1975, the biggest hit in Indian history for decades), and of a new "parallel cinema" (Shyam Benegal's Ankur, 1974). Doordarshan reached Bombay (1972) and beamed education by satellite to 2,400 villages in the SITE experiment (1975-76). India's first satellite, Aryabhata, was launched by the Soviet Union in April 1975. In sport, India won cricket series in the West Indies and England in 1971 and the hockey World Cup in 1975.

The ups

  • 1971: the only decisive military victory in India's history, a new nation created, 93,000 prisoners taken, and a refugee crisis of ten million resolved.
  • Food self-sufficiency: from "ship to mouth" to buffer stocks by the mid-1970s.
  • Banks reached villages; the poor got credit and savings accounts.
  • The nuclear test and the Soviet treaty made India a power that could not be bullied.
  • Punjab, Haryana, Himachal and the Northeast states were created; Sikkim joined.
  • Environmental law, child nutrition and women's rights legislation began.

The downs

  • The Emergency: 110,000 jailed, the press muzzled, forced sterilisation, the Constitution bent to protect one person.
  • The destruction of the Congress as a party with internal democracy; the beginning of the "high command" and dynasty culture copied by almost every Indian party since.
  • The economy: 3.6% growth, record inflation, record poverty; controls (MRTP, FERA, licences) at their most suffocating.
  • Politicising the judiciary, the civil service ("committed bureaucracy"), and the Governors.
  • Aizawl bombed; the Naxalite movement crushed with extrajudicial killings in Bengal.
  • Simla: a full victory converted into no settlement.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

  • Resigned in June 1975, appointed a stand-in, and appealed the court verdict. Many colleagues urged this. India would have been spared the Emergency and she might have returned as a hero.
  • Used 1971's popularity for reform instead of more controls. With a 352-seat majority and food security achieved, she had the political capital to loosen the Licence Raj twenty years before 1991. She tightened it instead.
  • Pushed for a Kashmir settlement at Simla while holding 93,000 prisoners. Accounts differ on whether Bhutto promised to convert the Line of Control into a border and reneged; either way, the leverage was never used again.
  • Kept bank nationalisation but not political lending, which created the bad-loan habit.
  • Handled JP's movement through Parliament and elections rather than treating it as sedition.

Could have been worse

  • She could have refused to hold the 1977 election, or cancelled it when the results came in. She did neither, and handed over power peacefully. Many dictators of that decade did not.
  • In 1971 she could have rushed to war in April, as the public demanded, and fought with the passes open to China and the monsoon flooding Bengal. She waited for the army's timetable.
  • The 1966-67 drought could have become a famine; it did not.
  • The nuclear option could have been abandoned under pressure; it was kept.

What it chose

Personal power over party and institutions; socialism as a political weapon more than an economic plan; food security over industrial growth; the Soviet Union over the West; and, in 1975, her own survival over the Constitution. Then, in 1977, the Constitution over her survival.

Government 4 · 24 March 1977 to 28 July 1979

Morarji Desai: the first non-Congress government

An 81-year-old Gandhian puritan led a party stitched together in a hurry from four groups that agreed on nothing except that Indira Gandhi must go. It repaired the Constitution, restored freedom, enjoyed two good harvests, and then tore itself apart in 28 months. Its collapse gave India a lasting distrust of coalitions.

Janata Party, a merger of the old Congress (O), the Hindu nationalist Jana Sangh, Charan Singh's Bharatiya Lok Dal, the Socialists, and Jagjivan Ram's Congress for Democracy

Majority (Janata and allies won about 330 of 542 seats; Congress fell to 154). The majority existed only on paper: the factions kept separate loyalties and leaders.

What it inherited

A country emerging from the Emergency with a huge appetite for freedom, an economy in surprisingly good shape (inflation near zero, foreign reserves at a record, a good monsoon coming), and a mandate to undo the previous two years. Also: a party whose leaders had spent their lives fighting each other, three of whom (Desai, Charan Singh, Jagjivan Ram) each believed he should be prime minister.

The conditions it worked under

Two good monsoons in 1977 and 1978 made it the luckiest government of the era on the weather. Then 1979 brought the worst drought in decades and the second oil shock (the Iranian revolution tripled the oil price again). Abroad, the Cold War briefly thawed under US President Carter, then froze again when the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan in December 1979.

What the government did

Repairing democracy

  • The 44th Amendment (1978), the most important thing the government did: an Emergency can now be declared only for "armed rebellion", not "internal disturbance"; it needs the written advice of the whole cabinet and approval by a two-thirds majority of Parliament within a month; the right to life and liberty can never be suspended; courts can again review laws; Parliament's term went back to five years. It also removed the right to property from the list of Fundamental Rights (so land reform could not be blocked by courts), a step Janata took, ironically, to finish Indira's socialist agenda.
  • The Shah Commission investigated the Emergency in public hearings and produced three damning reports (1978) on the arrests, the sterilisations and the demolitions. MISA, the preventive detention law, was repealed. Press censorship ended and the Press Council was revived.
  • The mistakes: Indira Gandhi was arrested in October 1977 on weak charges and released by a magistrate the next day, then jailed for a week in December 1978 after being expelled from Parliament. Both episodes made her a martyr. In April 1977 the government dismissed nine Congress state governments under Article 356, exactly the abuse it had condemned. And prosecutions of Emergency crimes mostly collapsed in court.

Economy

  • The Janata tilt was Gandhian and rural: more money for villages, 800-plus products reserved for small-scale industry, the Food for Work programme (1977) that paid rural labourers in grain from the overflowing stocks, and the Antyodaya ("the last person first") scheme for the poorest families.
  • Industry minister George Fernandes enforced the FERA law so strictly that Coca-Cola and IBM left India (1977-78) rather than hand over majority ownership and secret formulas. Coke returned in 1993; IBM in 1992.
  • In January 1978 the government demonetised the Rs 1,000, 5,000 and 10,000 notes to hit black money. Few ordinary people held such notes, so the effect was tiny; it is remembered mainly because of 2016.
  • Results: growth of 7.5% in 1977-78 and 5.5% in 1978-79, low inflation, and foreign reserves of about $7 billion, the best in India's history to that point. Then the 1979 drought and oil shock arrived, and 1979-80 saw the economy shrink 5.2%.

Society and religion

  • The Mandal Commission was appointed on 1 January 1979 under B.P. Mandal to identify the "backward classes" and recommend measures for them. Its report (December 1980) would sit in a drawer for a decade and then transform Indian politics.
  • Communal violence returned after the quiet of the Emergency: Aligarh (1978), Sambhal (1978), and Jamshedpur (April 1979, over 100 killed after a Ram Navami procession). The government's Jana Sangh members and their RSS links were blamed by the socialists in the party, which fed the "dual membership" quarrel.
  • Belchi (May 1977): 11 Dalit labourers were burned alive by upper-caste landlords in a Bihar village. Indira Gandhi rode to the village on an elephant through flooded roads. The photograph began her comeback.
  • Desai's personal crusades, total prohibition of alcohol and his advocacy of drinking one's own urine for health, made him a figure of fun and distracted from the government's real work.

Foreign policy

Vajpayee as foreign minister pursued "genuine non-alignment": relations with the United States warmed (Carter visited in January 1978), with Pakistan's new ruler Zia-ul-Haq they were correct, and Vajpayee made the first ministerial visit to China since 1962 (February 1979), cut short when China attacked Vietnam during his stay. Desai said India would not build a nuclear bomb, and Pakistan's programme, now backed by a lenient America, moved ahead.

The collapse

The party's three big men never stopped fighting. Charan Singh wanted the prime ministership and the Home Ministry's power to prosecute Indira; Desai sacked him in June 1978, reinstated him as Finance Minister in January 1979, and lost him again. The socialists demanded that former Jana Sangh ministers resign from the RSS ("dual membership"); they refused. In July 1979 Charan Singh walked out with his followers, Jagjivan Ram's supporters wavered, and Desai resigned without facing the no-confidence vote. India's first coalition had lasted 28 months.

The ups

  • Democracy was restored completely, and the 44th Amendment made a repeat of 1975 nearly impossible.
  • The Emergency's abuses were documented in public.
  • The economy had two of its best years; reserves hit a record.
  • Relations with the US, China and Pakistan improved at the same time.
  • The Mandal Commission, Food for Work and Antyodaya began long-lasting policies.

The downs

  • Chronic infighting; no coherent policy; collapse in 28 months.
  • Dismissing nine state governments; the bungled arrests of Indira Gandhi.
  • Communal riots returned; the RSS question split the party.
  • Coca-Cola and IBM's exit signalled to the world that India was closed for business.
  • The failure made voters distrust coalitions and brought Indira back stronger than before.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

A real merger with one leader and one programme, instead of a federation of egos; pursuing the Emergency's crimes through ordinary courts rather than headline arrests; leaving the Congress state governments alone; and using the good years and the big majority for economic reform, which no one in the party was interested in.

Could have been worse

The government could have used the Shah Commission's findings for a vendetta that undermined the courts, or kept the Emergency's powers for its own use. It did neither. The Constitution came out stronger than it had been before 1975.

What it chose

Freedom first, government second. It repaired the rules and then failed to govern by them.

What changed after

The 44th Amendment has protected Indian democracy for nearly fifty years. The Janata experiment's collapse discredited anti-Congress unity until 1989, and the Jana Sangh faction left in 1980 to form the Bharatiya Janata Party, which would rule India from 1998. Mandal's report waited for V.P. Singh. And Indira Gandhi, written off in 1977, won the January 1980 election by a landslide.

Charan Singh: the prime minister who never faced Parliament

The farmers' champion of north India got the job he had wanted all his life, with Indira Gandhi's support, and lost it 24 days later when she withdrew that support before he could prove his majority. He stayed on as caretaker for five more months while the economy sank into drought and the second oil shock.

Janata Party (Secular), later the Lok Dal

What it inherited and faced

The 1979 monsoon failed badly; the economy shrank 5.2% in 1979-80. Oil prices tripled after the Iranian revolution; inflation reached 17% in 1979-80 and 18% the next year. On 24 December 1979 the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan, making Pakistan an American "frontline state" and bringing the Cold War to India's border.

What it did

Almost nothing could be done by a caretaker. Charan Singh's real legacy came from before: as the leading voice of the peasant proprietor he had killed Nehru's cooperative farming plan in 1959, written the laws that abolished zamindari in Uttar Pradesh, and argued for decades that India should develop through agriculture and small industry rather than big factories. His birthday, 23 December, is marked as National Farmers' Day. As prime minister he presided over the drought relief effort and prepared for an election he was certain to lose.

The ups

  • A peaceful transfer of power through a fresh election, despite a messy collapse.
  • The dignity of farmers put at the centre of national politics for the first time.

The downs

  • A government formed by splitting one's own party, dependent on the person it had been formed to oppose.
  • Five months of drift during a severe economic shock.

Could have done better

Refused Indira Gandhi's poisoned offer of support, or faced the vote and lost honourably in the House.

Could have been worse

Singh could have tried to cling on by other means. He did not; the election came on time.

What it chose

Ambition over arithmetic, then a quiet exit.

What changed after

The January 1980 election returned Indira Gandhi with 353 seats. Charan Singh's Lok Dal became the seed of the farmer-caste politics of north India; his political heirs (Mulayam Singh Yadav, Ajit Singh, and in a different way Devi Lal) dominated Uttar Pradesh and Haryana for decades. The lesson that "outside support" is a trap was learned again in 1990, 1991 and 1997.

Assassinated by two of her Sikh bodyguards at her residence on 31 October 1984, five months after ordering the army into the Golden Temple.

What she inherited

A shrinking economy (-5.2% in 1979-80), inflation near 18%, oil prices tripled, a Soviet army in Afghanistan, an agitation in Assam against Bangladeshi migrants that had paralysed the state, Akali demands in Punjab, and a Congress that was now entirely her own: no Syndicate, no strong state leaders, nobody to say no.

The conditions she worked under

  • The second oil shock and a world recession (1980-82) squeezed foreign exchange; the balance of payments turned sharply negative.
  • Afghanistan made Pakistan the West's favourite: a $3.2 billion American package including F-16 jets (1981), and a quiet decision in Washington to ignore Pakistan's nuclear programme. India read this as encirclement.
  • Gulf money began to flow: millions of Indian workers went to the Gulf after the oil booms, and their remittances became a permanent cushion for the rupee.
  • Identity politics was rising everywhere: Sikh, Assamese, Tamil (in Sri Lanka), Kashmiri, and a new Hindu assertiveness after the mass conversion of Dalits to Islam at Meenakshipuram in Tamil Nadu (1981).

What the government did

The economy: a quiet change of direction

In simple words

Without ever saying so, the government that had nationalised everything in 1969 started loosening its grip in 1980: fewer licences for some industries, easier imports of machinery, a car made with a Japanese partner, colour TV. The economy responded: growth averaged about 5.7% a year, breaking the "Hindu rate" of 3.5% for the first time in 30 years. Economists call this "liberalisation by stealth". The catch: it was financed by borrowing.

  • The IMF loan (1981): India took the largest loan the IMF had ever given, about $5.8 billion (SDR 5 billion), to ride out the oil shock. The conditions were mild; India drew about three-quarters and repaid early (1984). It was politically brave, since borrowing from the IMF was seen as surrender to the West, and it worked.
  • Licensing eased: some industries were freed from licences, firms were allowed to make related products without fresh permission ("broad-banding"), cement and some steel prices were decontrolled (1982), and imports of capital goods and technology were made easier.
  • Maruti: Sanjay Gandhi's failed car company was revived with Suzuki of Japan as partner (1982); the Maruti 800 rolled out in December 1983 and changed the Indian middle class's idea of what it could own. Colour television arrived for the 1982 Asian Games.
  • Six more banks were nationalised (April 1980), and new rural institutions were created: NABARD (1982) for farm credit and the Exim Bank (1982) for trade. Anti-poverty schemes (IRDP, the rural employment programmes) grew.
  • The bill: the government's deficit widened steadily; by the mid-1980s it was being financed by printing money and foreign borrowing. The 1991 crisis has its roots here.
  • Bombay's great textile strike (1982-83), led by Datta Samant, kept 250,000 workers out for 18 months, destroyed the city's mill industry, and shifted textiles to the unorganised "powerloom" towns.

Politics: everything through Delhi

  • In February 1980 she dismissed nine opposition state governments, repaying Janata's 1977 move in kind. Governors became instruments: in 1984 the Governor of Andhra Pradesh dismissed N.T. Rama Rao's hugely popular government while he was abroad for surgery (public fury forced his reinstatement within a month), and in Kashmir Governor Jagmohan replaced Farooq Abdullah's elected government with a defector's. The Kashmir dismissal, like the 1953 arrest of his father, convinced many Kashmiris that their votes did not count.
  • Sanjay Gandhi died in June 1980 flying a stunt plane. Her elder son Rajiv, an airline pilot with no interest in politics, was drafted in 1981 and was general secretary of the party by 1983. The dynasty became permanent.
  • Assam: the All Assam Students' Union had agitated since 1979 to remove "foreigners" (migrants from Bangladesh) from the voter rolls. In February 1983 she forced a state election that the agitators boycotted. In the violence that followed, at Nellie, about 2,200 Bengali-speaking Muslims were massacred in a single morning by surrounding villagers. No one was ever convicted.

Punjab: from manipulation to Blue Star

  • 1978-80 Congress leaders in Punjab (with Sanjay Gandhi and Zail Singh's involvement) promote a fiery young preacher, Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale, to split the Sikh vote of the rival Akali Dal. The strategy works, then escapes control.
  • 1981 A newspaper owner, Lala Jagat Narain, is murdered; Bhindranwale is arrested and released. Killings of Hindus and of Sikh officials begin.
  • 1982-83 The Akalis launch an agitation for their Anandpur Sahib demands (river water, Chandigarh, more autonomy). Talks with the government repeatedly near a deal and collapse, often because Delhi worries about the Hindu vote in Haryana and the coming general election. Bhindranwale moves into the Golden Temple complex; a senior police officer is shot dead on its steps (April 1983); bus passengers are pulled out and shot by religion (October 1983). President's Rule is imposed.
  • December 1983 Bhindranwale occupies the Akal Takht, the holiest seat of Sikh authority, and fortifies it with the help of a cashiered general.
  • 1-8 June 1984 Operation Blue Star: the army, with tanks, storms the Golden Temple. Bhindranwale is killed; the Akal Takht is badly damaged; the Sikh reference library burns. Official figures: 83 soldiers and 493 "civilians and militants" dead; independent estimates run far higher, since the complex was full of pilgrims on a holy day. Sikh soldiers mutiny in several units. Sikh opinion worldwide is traumatised.
  • 31 October 1984 Beant Singh and Satwant Singh, Sikh members of her own security detail, shoot her dead in her garden.
  • 31 October to 3 November Mobs, in many cases led by Congress politicians with voter lists and police standing by, kill about 2,700 Sikhs in Delhi (official count; many estimates are higher) and over 3,000 across India. Convictions of leaders took 34 years.

Foreign policy and security

  • India did not condemn the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (it abstained at the UN), which cost it credibility among non-aligned countries and in Washington, though relations with the Reagan administration improved later (her 1982 US visit; the Tarapur nuclear fuel dispute was settled through France).
  • She hosted the Non-Aligned summit (1983) and the Commonwealth summit (1983) in Delhi, a high point of India's diplomatic profile.
  • Sri Lanka: after the July 1983 anti-Tamil pogrom in Colombo, India began training and arming Tamil militant groups, including the LTTE, through its intelligence agency. This decision would kill her son.
  • Siachen (April 1984): learning that Pakistan planned to occupy the uninhabited Siachen glacier, India got there first ("Operation Meghdoot"). The world's highest battlefield has been held, at great cost in lives and money, ever since.
  • Reports of an Indian plan to bomb Pakistan's Kahuta nuclear plant in 1984 (possibly with Israeli help) were never confirmed; the plan, if it existed, was shelved.

Society and culture

  • The women's movement won its first legal victories: after the Mathura custodial rape case, the 1983 amendment shifted the burden of proof in custodial rape; dowry laws were tightened (1984, 1986) after a wave of "bride burnings" in Delhi.
  • Communal violence rose: Moradabad (1980, police firing on Eid prayers, hundreds dead), Biharsharif (1981), Bhiwandi (1984). The Vishva Hindu Parishad's "Ekatmata Yatra" (1983) began the mass mobilisation that would lead to Ayodhya.
  • The 1982 Asian Games rebuilt Delhi (flyovers, stadiums, hotels) and brought colour TV to Indian homes. Doordarshan's first soap opera, Hum Log, began in July 1984. Richard Attenborough's Gandhi (1982) swept the Oscars; Bhanu Athaiya became the first Indian Oscar winner for its costumes. Salman Rushdie's Midnight's Children won the Booker Prize (1981) and announced Indian English writing to the world.
  • On 25 June 1983 Kapil Dev's team won the cricket World Cup at Lord's against the mighty West Indies. Cricket became India's religion. In April 1984 Rakesh Sharma became the first Indian in space, aboard a Soviet craft; asked how India looked from above, he answered "Saare jahan se achha" (better than the whole world). India's first communication satellite INSAT-1B (1983) worked after its predecessor failed.

The ups

  • Growth above 5.5% a year, the first sustained break from the "Hindu rate".
  • The IMF loan managed without a crisis; cautious economic opening; Maruti, colour TV, satellites.
  • Siachen secured; the NAM and Commonwealth summits; a thaw with Washington.
  • Women's rights legislation; the start of mass TV; the 1983 World Cup.

The downs

  • Punjab: creating Bhindranwale, refusing available settlements, and then Blue Star. Thousands dead, a decade of insurgency, a wound in Sikh memory that has not closed.
  • Assam's forced election and the Nellie massacre.
  • Dismissing elected governments in Kashmir and Andhra; the Governors as party agents.
  • Deficits that led to 1991; the Bombay mills destroyed.
  • Arming Tamil militants in Sri Lanka.
  • The dynasty entrenched with Rajiv's induction.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

  • Settled with the moderate Akalis in 1981-83. At least three near-agreements were abandoned for electoral reasons. Every demand that was conceded in the 1985 Punjab Accord had been on the table in 1982.
  • Never promoted Bhindranwale, and arrested him in 1981-82 when it was still a police matter, before he moved into the Golden Temple.
  • Postponed Assam's 1983 election and settled the voter-roll question first, as the 1985 Accord eventually did.
  • Left Farooq Abdullah's and NTR's governments alone.
  • Financed growth with reform rather than debt.

Could have been worse

Blue Star could have been even bloodier; some advisers wanted a long siege. She could have ignored the IMF route and let the 1980 crisis become a 1991-style collapse. She could have let Pakistan take Siachen. And she did not, this time, reach for an Emergency.

What it chose

Centralised power, short-term electoral advantage in the states, cautious opening of the economy, and, when manipulation failed, force.

Government 7 · 31 October 1984 to 2 December 1989

Rajiv Gandhi: the 21st-century modernizer who lost his way

A 40-year-old pilot with the biggest majority in Indian history, Rajiv Gandhi promised to take India into the 21st century: computers, telephones, clean politics, peace in Punjab, Assam and Mizoram. Much of that happened. But by 1989 a weapons scandal, two religious blunders, a war in Sri Lanka and a mounting debt had squandered the mandate, and he lost.

Party
Congress (I)
Type of government
The largest majority ever: 404 of the 514 seats contested in December 1984 (415 of 541 after delayed polls), with 48% of the vote, on a wave of sympathy after his mother's murder.
Key people
V.P. Singh (Finance, then Defence; sacked 1987), Arun Nehru and Arun Singh (close advisers, later estranged), Sam Pitroda (telecom), P.V. Narasimha Rao (Home, HRD), Buta Singh (Home), N.D. What he inherited

A country in shock, a party guilty of the anti-Sikh massacres (his own remark, "when a big tree falls, the earth shakes", was taken as excusing them), an insurgency in Punjab, a six-year agitation in Assam, a 20-year rebellion in Mizoram, a Kashmir resentful after the 1984 dismissal, and a growing economy running on borrowed money. Two days after he took office the world's worst industrial disaster struck: on the night of 2-3 December 1984, poison gas leaked from the Union Carbide pesticide plant in Bhopal, killing about 3,800 people immediately by the official count (estimates of total deaths run to 15,000-20,000) and injuring half a million. The company's American chairman was arrested, released on bail and allowed to leave India within days.

The conditions he worked under

  • A goodwill wave at home and abroad: young, clean, modern, with an unassailable majority. Everything was possible for about two years.
  • Technology was changing: personal computers, digital telephone exchanges, satellite television. India could join or be left behind.
  • The Cold War was ending: Gorbachev came to power in 1985; the Soviet Union, India's protector and biggest arms supplier, was weakening. The United States under Reagan was friendlier but suspicious.
  • Religious politics were rising on all sides: Sikh militancy, Muslim anxiety after the Shah Bano case, and a Hindu campaign for a temple at Ayodhya.
  • Oil prices collapsed in 1986, which helped the import bill but cut Gulf remittances.

What the government did

Peace accords, 1985-86: the best year

  • Punjab Accord (July 1985) with Akali leader Sant Longowal: Chandigarh to Punjab, a water tribunal, an inquiry into the 1984 killings, and elections. Longowal was assassinated by militants three weeks later; the Akalis won the election; the transfer of Chandigarh never happened; the insurgency went on. But the accord was a serious attempt.
  • Assam Accord (August 1985): migrants who had arrived before 1966 would stay, those from 1966-71 would be regularised, those after 24 March 1971 would be detected and expelled. The students' leaders won the election and governed. Expulsions never really happened, which is why the National Register of Citizens exercise of 2019 and the Citizenship Amendment Act of 2019 are still fighting this battle.
  • Mizoram Accord (June 1986): the rebel leader Laldenga came home, Mizoram became a state (1987), and he became its chief minister. One of the most successful peace settlements anywhere; Mizoram has been at peace since.
  • Smaller deals followed in Tripura (1988) and with the Gorkhaland movement in Darjeeling (1988).

Politics and institutions

  • The anti-defection law (52nd Amendment, 1985) disqualified legislators who changed parties. It ended the open buying of legislators but, critics say, made MPs mere voting machines for party bosses.
  • The voting age was cut from 21 to 18 (1988-89).
  • Panchayati Raj: his bills to give constitutional status to village and city councils (1989) failed in the Rajya Sabha, where Congress lacked a majority; Narasimha Rao passed them in 1992. His line that "only 15 paise of every rupee" sent from Delhi reached the poor became famous.
  • Against his own party: at the Congress centenary (December 1985) he attacked the "power brokers" who had captured the party. Nothing came of it; the power brokers were his MPs.
  • The Defamation Bill (1988), which would have made it easier to jail journalists, was withdrawn after nationwide press protests. His relations with President Zail Singh collapsed (the President sat on a bill allowing the government to open people's mail, and was rumoured to be considering dismissing him).

The economy: a modern streak, financed by debt

In simple words

Rajiv's team cut income tax, relaxed licences on electronics, computers and telecom, and let in foreign technology. Growth stayed above 5.5% and a consumer class appeared: Maruti cars, colour TVs, computers, telephones that worked. But the government spent far more than it earned, year after year, and borrowed abroad in short-term loans. By 1989 India was heading for a crash.

  • The 1985 budget (V.P. Singh) cut the top income tax rate from 62% to 50%, cut corporate taxes, abolished estate duty, and promised stable tax rules. Licences were relaxed in 25 industries, and the rules on company size (MRTP) were loosened.
  • Telecom: Sam Pitroda's C-DOT built cheap digital exchanges that could survive Indian heat and dust; the yellow-signboard STD/PCO booths put long-distance calling in every village. MTNL and VSNL were set up (1986). The telephone waiting list, which had been a decade long, started to shrink.
  • Computers: the 1984-86 computer policies cut duties and let in imports; the railways' computerised reservation system (1986) was a revelation to ordinary Indians. When the United States refused to sell India a Cray supercomputer, C-DAC (1988) built its own, PARAM, by 1991. The six "technology missions" (drinking water, literacy, immunisation, oilseeds, telecom, dairy) brought project management into welfare.
  • The reforms stalled by 1987 after V.P. Singh's tax raids on big business houses frightened industrialists and party financiers; Singh was shifted to Defence, and the "raid raj" ended along with the reform momentum.
  • The numbers: growth averaged 5.7% a year (10% in the bumper year 1988-89). The central government's fiscal deficit went above 8% of GDP; external debt doubled; the current account deficit widened. Inflation was 8-9%. The stock market index (Sensex) was created in 1986 and Reliance Industries, which had learnt to work the Licence Raj better than anyone, became the symbol of the new corporate India, and of its coziness with power.

Religion: the two blunders

  • Shah Bano (1985-86): the Supreme Court ruled that Shah Bano, a 62-year-old divorced Muslim woman, was entitled to maintenance from her husband under the general criminal law, as any Indian woman was. Conservative Muslim leaders protested that this violated Islamic personal law. Rajiv, with his 400 seats, passed the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986, which overturned the judgment. Reformist Muslims, women's groups and Hindu nationalists all condemned it; "appeasement" became the BJP's most effective word for the next forty years.
  • Ayodhya (1986): to balance the Hindu anger, within weeks a district court in Faizabad ordered the locks on the Babri Masjid opened so Hindus could worship at the idols placed inside in 1949. Government and Congress officials were widely believed to have arranged it. The Vishva Hindu Parishad's campaign for a Ram temple on the site now had an open door. In November 1989, days before the election, the government allowed the VHP to lay a foundation stone (shilanyas) next to the mosque. Rajiv began his campaign from Ayodhya promising "Ram Rajya".
  • Riots: Meerut (1987), where the Provincial Armed Constabulary shot 42 Muslim men at Hashimpura and dumped the bodies in a canal (convictions came in 2018), and Bhagalpur (October 1989), where about 1,000 people, mostly Muslims, were killed over weeks and the police were complicit. The Sikh massacres of 1984 were investigated by a commission (Misra, 1987) that blamed no one of importance.
  • Sati: in September 1987 an 18-year-old widow, Roop Kanwar, was burned on her husband's pyre in Rajasthan before a crowd that celebrated it. The Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act followed within months.

Bofors and the fall

In April 1987 Swedish Radio reported that the Swedish arms maker Bofors had paid about Rs 64 crore in bribes to win a 1986 contract for 410 field guns. The names pointed to people close to the prime minister, including an Italian businessman friend of the family, Ottavio Quattrocchi. Rajiv denied everything, and the investigation was obstructed for decades (no one was ever convicted; the guns themselves performed superbly in Kargil in 1999). But "Mr Clean" was finished. V.P. Singh, sacked from Defence for pursuing the affair, became the leader of the opposition, and "Bofors" the one-word slogan that beat Congress in 1989.

Foreign policy and security

  • Sri Lanka: the Indo-Sri Lanka Accord (July 1987) was meant to end the civil war by giving Tamils autonomy and disarming the Tamil Tigers (LTTE), with an Indian Peace Keeping Force to supervise. The LTTE refused to disarm. The IPKF, 100,000 strong at its peak, ended up fighting the Tigers India had once armed; about 1,200 Indian soldiers died and the force withdrew in 1990 with nothing achieved. In 1991 the LTTE killed Rajiv in revenge.
  • Pakistan: "Operation Brasstacks" (1986-87), a giant army exercise near the border, nearly triggered a war; Pakistan's A.Q. Khan used the scare to announce that Pakistan had the bomb. Relations later warmed with Benazir Bhutto (1988).
  • China: after a tense standoff at Sumdorong Chu (1986-87), Rajiv visited Beijing in December 1988, the first prime minister to go since 1954, and began the border talks and trade that defined the next thirty years.
  • Maldives: Indian paratroopers crushed a mercenary coup within hours (Operation Cactus, 1988), winning praise worldwide.
  • Nuclear: he proposed a world free of nuclear weapons at the UN (1988) while quietly authorising India's weapons programme to produce a deliverable bomb; the Prithvi (1988) and Agni (1989) missiles were first tested in his term.

Society and culture

  • The National Policy on Education (1986) created the Navodaya residential schools for rural talent and "Operation Blackboard" to give every primary school basic equipment; the National Literacy Mission (1988) ran adult literacy campaigns. The Environment Protection Act (1986) was passed in response to Bhopal, and the Ganga Action Plan (1986) began the never-finished clean-up of the river. The Jawahar Rozgar Yojana (1989) became the main rural jobs scheme until it grew into MGNREGA.
  • Television conquered India. Doordarshan's Ramayan (1987-88) and Mahabharat (1988-90) emptied the streets every Sunday morning; the serials made Hindu epics a shared national television experience and, many argue, prepared the ground for the Ayodhya movement. Buniyaad, Nukkad and the news magazine shows created a national audience. The "Mile Sur Mera Tumhara" national integration song (1988) is still played.
  • Pepsi was allowed in (1988-90) in a joint venture with Punjab farmers, a sign of change. Reliance's annual general meetings filled stadiums.
  • In sport: India won the World Championship of Cricket in Australia (1985) and co-hosted the 1987 World Cup; P.T. Usha won four golds at the 1986 Asian Games; Viswanathan Anand became India's first chess grandmaster (1988); a 16-year-old Sachin Tendulkar made his Test debut in Pakistan (November 1989).

The ups

  • Peace accords in Punjab, Assam and Mizoram; Mizoram's is a model.
  • Telecom and computers: the technological foundation of the 1990s IT boom.
  • Tax cuts and licence easing that kept growth near 6% and created a consumer class.
  • The anti-defection law, the 18-year vote, education and environment laws.
  • Breakthroughs with China and in the Maldives; a credible missile programme.

The downs

  • Shah Bano and Ayodhya: trying to please both sides, he empowered the hardliners on each. The road to the 1992 demolition starts here.
  • Bofors, and the cover-up that was worse than the crime.
  • Sri Lanka: 1,200 soldiers dead in a war against a group India had armed.
  • Fiscal recklessness that led directly to the 1991 crisis.
  • Bhopal: the company's chairman let go; a 1989 settlement of $470 million that victims considered a betrayal.
  • Bhagalpur, Hashimpura, and the failure to punish the 1984 killers.
  • Kashmir's rigged 1987 state election, after which the militancy began.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

  • With 400 seats, 1991 could have happened in 1985. He had the majority to dismantle the Licence Raj fully, reform taxes, open trade and fix the budget. He did a quarter of it and then stopped when business and party money complained.
  • Let the Shah Bano judgment stand, as reformers in his own party and many Muslims urged, and left the Ayodhya locks shut. Both decisions were made for votes he lost anyway.
  • Come clean on Bofors. A quick inquiry in 1987 would have hurt; the decade of cover-up destroyed the Congress's moral claim.
  • Kept the army out of Sri Lanka, or sent it with a clear mission and a date to leave.
  • Prosecuted the 1984 killers in his own party.

Could have been worse

He could have used 400 seats to rewrite the Constitution, as his mother did with 352. He did not. He kept the peace deals even when they cost him politically, accepted a hostile President, withdrew the press law when challenged, and handed over power gracefully in 1989. And the technological turn, if late and partial, was real.

What it chose

Modernity in technology, caution in economics, opportunism in religion, and loyalty to friends over accountability.

VPS

Government 8 · 2 December 1989 to 10 November 1990

V.P. Singh: Mandal, Kashmir, and the year everything changed

The man who brought down Rajiv Gandhi over Bofors lasted eleven months, propped up by the Hindu right and the communist left at the same time. In that short time he lost control of Kashmir, set off a caste revolution with the Mandal Commission, watched a chariot roll towards Ayodhya, and left an economy on the edge of default. Few governments have mattered more for their length.

What he inherited

A mandate to be clean, a treasury that was nearly empty (the fiscal deficit above 8%, foreign debt at $70 billion), Punjab at its most violent, Ayodhya turned into a national campaign by the 1989 shilanyas, and a Kashmir where a rigged 1987 election had sent a generation of young men across the border for arms training. His coalition could be ended at any moment by either of its two very different supporters.

The conditions he worked under

  • The Gulf War: Iraq invaded Kuwait in August 1990; the oil price doubled, 170,000 Indians in Kuwait had to be airlifted home (Air India flew nearly 500 flights, a world record), and the remittances they had sent stopped. India's foreign reserves began to drain away.
  • The Soviet collapse: the Berlin Wall fell a month before he took office. India's main arms supplier, trading partner and protector was disintegrating.
  • Pakistan's new strategy: having bled the Soviets in Afghanistan with American help, Pakistan's intelligence service turned the same methods on Kashmir, with training camps, weapons and a flood of recruits.

What the government did

Kashmir: the insurgency explodes

  • 8 December 1989 Militants kidnap Rubaiya Sayeed, daughter of the new Home Minister, six days into the government. After five days the government releases five jailed militants in exchange. Crowds in Srinagar celebrate; the militants' prestige soars. Many officials, then and since, see this as the moment the state lost its nerve.
  • 19 January 1990 Jagmohan, a hardliner, is appointed Governor; Chief Minister Farooq Abdullah resigns in protest. That night, house-to-house searches in Srinagar provoke mass protests.
  • 21 January At the Gawkadal bridge, paramilitary forces fire on a procession; at least 50 are killed. Srinagar becomes a city of curfews and funerals.
  • January to March After targeted killings of Hindu officials and threats broadcast from mosques, the Kashmiri Pandit community, a Hindu minority of about 150,000, flees the valley almost entirely. The state neither protects them nor stops the exodus. Most still live in Jammu and Delhi; it is one of the unresolved wounds of the republic.
  • May 1990 The moderate religious leader Mirwaiz Farooq is assassinated; security forces fire on his funeral procession. By mid-year the valley is in full insurgency, the AFSPA is extended to Kashmir, and the conflict that would kill over 40,000 people in the next two decades has begun.

Mandal: the caste earthquake

In simple words

The Mandal report of 1980 had said that "Other Backward Classes", the farming and artisan castes between the upper castes and the Dalits, were about half the population but held only a tiny share of government jobs, and recommended reserving 27% of central jobs for them. For ten years no government touched it. On 7 August 1990, V.P. Singh announced he would implement it, two days before his sacked deputy Devi Lal planned a huge farmers' rally against him. Supporters called it social justice; critics called it a cynical move to split his rival's base. It was both.

  • Upper-caste students in north India erupted. Over 200 attempted self-immolation and about 60 died; Rajeev Goswami, a Delhi University student who set himself on fire on 19 September, became the symbol. Buses burned, universities closed.
  • The Supreme Court stayed the order in October 1990 and, in the Indra Sawhney judgment (November 1992), upheld OBC reservation while excluding the "creamy layer" (richer OBC families) and capping all reservations at 50%. The quota took effect in 1993.
  • The deeper effect was political: OBC voters, now a self-aware block, flocked to leaders of their own castes. Lalu Yadav in Bihar, Mulayam Singh Yadav in Uttar Pradesh, Nitish Kumar, Sharad Yadav, and later dozens of regional parties built what the scholar Christophe Jaffrelot called "India's silent revolution": the transfer of power in the north from upper castes to the middle castes. The BJP would spend two decades learning to win OBC votes back, and eventually succeed under Narendra Modi, himself from an OBC community.

Ayodhya: the Rath Yatra

Fearing that Mandal would split the Hindu vote by caste, the BJP answered with religion. On 25 September 1990, L.K. Advani set out from the Somnath temple in a Toyota decorated as a chariot, heading for Ayodhya to begin building the Ram temple on 30 October. Crowds and communal violence followed the route. On 23 October, Lalu Yadav had Advani arrested in Bihar. The BJP withdrew support from the government the same day. On 30 October and 2 November, Mulayam Singh Yadav's Uttar Pradesh police fired on volunteers ("kar sevaks") who stormed the mosque; between 16 and 50 died, and became martyrs for the movement. V.P. Singh lost the confidence vote on 7 November. The "Mandal versus Kamandal" (caste versus temple) contest that has shaped north Indian politics ever since was now fully formed.

Other acts

  • A visit to the Golden Temple within days of taking office, and the release of Punjab's political detainees, as gestures of healing to Sikhs. The insurgency, however, peaked in 1990-91.
  • Ambedkar was awarded the Bharat Ratna (1990) and his birthday became a holiday; the Scheduled Castes and Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act came into force.
  • The March 1990 budget waived farm loans up to Rs 10,000, Devi Lal's demand, at a cost of around Rs 10,000 crore the government did not have.
  • Foreign minister Gujral embraced Saddam Hussein in Baghdad days after the Kuwait invasion, an embarrassment that overshadowed the successful airlift.
  • The economy grew 5.3% in 1990-91, but by the time he left, reserves had fallen to about $1 billion, India's credit rating was being cut, and overseas Indians were pulling their deposits out of Indian banks.

The ups

  • OBC reservation: a long-delayed act of social justice that brought half the population into the state's jobs and, later, into power.
  • Gestures of reconciliation with Sikhs; honour for Ambedkar; the atrocities law.
  • The Kuwait airlift.

The downs

  • Kashmir: the hostage swap, the Gawkadal firing, the Pandit exodus, the slide into a 30-year insurgency.
  • Mandal's timing and manner: no consultation, no phasing, no creamy-layer rule, announced as a political strike. Sixty young people died.
  • The loan waiver and the drift towards default.
  • A coalition designed to collapse.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

  • Held firm on the Rubaiya kidnapping, as several cabinet members and officials urged; or at least not celebrated the swap.
  • Protected the Pandits, or organised their return, instead of letting a community disappear from its homeland.
  • Implemented Mandal through a parliamentary process with the creamy-layer exclusion the Supreme Court later added, and with equal attention to OBC education, not just jobs.
  • Gone to the IMF in 1990, before the reserves hit zero.

Could have been worse

The Rath Yatra could have reached Ayodhya in 1990 and the mosque could have fallen then, with the government's consent. Kashmir could have been handed to the army entirely. He could have given in to the BJP to survive. He did not; he lost office instead.

What it chose

Caste justice over coalition stability, and principle over survival in the final vote; but on Kashmir, hesitation over decision.

What changed after

Mandal made OBC parties the kingmakers of the 1990s and forced every party, including the BJP, to become a party of backward castes. Kashmir's insurgency defined India-Pakistan relations for thirty years. The Rath Yatra led to the 1992 demolition. And the empty treasury he left made the 1991 reforms unavoidable.

CSh

Government 9 · 10 November 1990 to 21 June 1991 (caretaker from 6 March 1991)

Chandra Shekhar: the caretaker who pawned the gold

A socialist rebel who had never held office took the prime ministership with 64 MPs and Congress's support, and found the country days away from being unable to pay for its oil. He kept India solvent, nearly settled Ayodhya, and was dropped by Congress over a petty spying allegation. His was the shortest full government in India's history and one of the least deserving of its obscurity.

Party
Samajwadi Janata Party (a breakaway of 64 MPs from the Janata Dal)
Type of government
Minority, with outside support from Congress (I), which withdrew it on 6 March 1991. He stayed as caretaker through the election, which Rajiv Gandhi's assassination interrupted.
Key people
Yashwant Sinha (Finance), Subramanian Swamy (Commerce and Law), Devi Lal (Deputy PM), S. Venkitaramanan (RBI Governor)
President
R. Venkataraman

What it inherited and faced

Foreign reserves of about $1 billion, enough for two weeks of imports, with oil prices doubled by the Gulf War (January-February 1991), overseas Indians withdrawing their deposits, foreign banks refusing to roll over short-term loans, and the credit-rating agencies downgrading India to junk. The real possibility of a sovereign default, the first in India's history, which would have cut it off from world finance for years.

What the government did

  • Kept the country solvent. It negotiated the first emergency IMF loans (about $1.8 billion in January 1991) and, in the step that became a national humiliation and a national memory, had the State Bank of India ship 20 tonnes of gold to Switzerland (sold with a buy-back option) to raise $200 million. (A further 47 tonnes went to the Bank of England in July 1991 under Rao.) Imports were slashed.
  • Prepared the reforms. Finance Minister Yashwant Sinha drafted a budget containing many of the measures that Manmohan Singh would announce in July 1991: cutting the deficit, easing licences, courting foreign investment. Congress would not let him present it; he could only pass a stop-gap "vote on account" in March.
  • Nearly settled Ayodhya. He brought the Vishva Hindu Parishad and the Babri Masjid Action Committee to the table in December 1990 and January 1991 to examine the historical evidence together, the closest the two sides ever came to a negotiated outcome. The talks died with his government.
  • Let American military aircraft refuel in Bombay during the Gulf War, a quiet break with non-alignment that Rajiv Gandhi attacked publicly, forcing a reversal.
  • Sent the army against the ULFA insurgents in Assam (Operation Bajrang, November 1990) and dismissed the state government.

The fall

In March 1991 two Haryana police constables were found watching Rajiv Gandhi's house. Congress called it spying and withdrew support. Chandra Shekhar resigned rather than be humiliated further, and the President dissolved Parliament. During the election campaign that followed, on 21 May 1991, Rajiv Gandhi was killed by a Tamil Tiger suicide bomber at Sriperumbudur in Tamil Nadu. The remaining polling was postponed; a sympathy wave carried Congress to 244 seats.

The ups

  • No default: the gold pledge and IMF loans bought the time that the 1991 reforms needed.
  • The groundwork for the reforms was laid here; Sinha and Venkitaramanan handed Rao and Manmohan Singh a ready plan.
  • The most serious Ayodhya negotiation ever attempted.

The downs

  • A government formed by splitting a party, with no mandate, lasting seven months.
  • The gold pledge, however necessary, was felt as national shame.
  • The refuelling episode showed a government too weak to make foreign policy.

Could have done better

Very little was possible with 64 MPs. A government with a real majority in 1990 could have started reforms a year earlier and avoided the gold shipments altogether.

Could have been worse

India could have defaulted. Several countries in Latin America had, in the 1980s, and lost a decade. It did not.

What it chose

Solvency over pride, and resignation over clinging on.

What changed after

The crisis he managed gave Narasimha Rao the political cover ("there is no alternative") to carry out the most important economic change since 1947. The gold came back; the memory of pledging it has been used by every finance minister since to argue for fiscal discipline.

PVN

Government 10 · 21 June 1991 to 16 May 1996

P.V. Narasimha Rao: the reformer by accident

A 70-year-old scholar who had packed his books to retire was made prime minister because the Congress's chosen leader had been assassinated. Leading a minority government, with the country about to default, he and his finance minister Manmohan Singh dismantled forty years of economic controls in a few months. The same government stood by while a mob destroyed the Babri Masjid. India's economy and India's politics both changed direction on his watch, in opposite moral directions.

Party
Indian National Congress
Type of government
Minority at first (244 of 521 seats contested in 1991; 232 on election day), surviving on the opposition's divisions and later on defections and a scandal-tainted vote; it reached a bare majority by 1993. The first government to serve a full term without a majority of its own.

What he inherited

Reserves of about $1 billion, gold already pawned, inflation at 14%, a fiscal deficit above 8% of GDP, a credit rating cut to junk, an IMF waiting with conditions, a Soviet Union months from dissolution, insurgencies in Kashmir, Punjab and Assam, the Ayodhya movement at full strength, and a party that did not want him and expected him to fail.

The conditions he worked under

  • The crisis itself was the opportunity. A finance ministry team (Ahluwalia's "M document" of 1990, Yashwant Sinha's shelved budget) already had a plan. The IMF wanted reforms. Big business wanted out of the Licence Raj. The public wanted anything but default. For once, the politics of reform lined up.
  • The end of the Cold War. The Soviet collapse (December 1991) removed India's patron and its bartered trade; the United States was the only superpower; China's reforms were visibly working; "socialism" had lost its prestige everywhere.
  • Religious politics at full force. The BJP now ruled Uttar Pradesh, the state where Ayodhya lies, and the VHP had a date for the temple.
  • No majority. Every reform had to be slipped past a hostile Parliament, and every crisis handled without the ability to simply outvote the opposition.

What the government did

The 1991 reforms

In simple words

Before 1991, a businessman needed permission to open a factory, to expand it, to import a machine, to raise money, to make a new product. Foreign companies were mostly kept out. Tariffs of 100-300% kept foreign goods out. The rupee's value was fixed by the government. In the summer of 1991 the government abolished most licences, cut tariffs, let foreign companies in, made the rupee cheaper and then let the market set its price, and sold shares in its own companies. The economy, like a compressed spring, jumped.

  • 1 and 3 July 1991 The rupee is devalued in two steps, by about 18% in total. Rao is said to have tried to stop the second step and been told it was already done.
  • 24 July 1991 The New Industrial Policy abolishes licensing for all but 18 industries (later 6), scraps the size limits on big companies, cuts the list of industries reserved for the state from 17 to 8 (later 3), and allows foreign ownership up to 51% automatically in 34 industries. The same evening Manmohan Singh presents a budget that cuts the deficit, cuts subsidies, and ends with Victor Hugo's line: "No power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come."
  • 1991-92 Import licensing is largely abolished; the peak tariff is cut from over 300% towards 150%. An IMF standby loan of about $2.2 billion and World Bank loans come through. The Narasimham Committee sets out banking reform.
  • 1992-94 Foreign institutional investors are allowed to buy Indian shares (1992); SEBI becomes the statutory stock-market regulator (1992) after the Harshad Mehta scandal; private mutual funds (1993) and new private banks (HDFC, ICICI, UTI Bank, IndusInd, 1994-95) are licensed; the National Stock Exchange begins electronic trading (1994); the rupee becomes convertible for trade (1994); the peak tariff falls to 50% (1995); corporate and personal tax rates are cut.
  • 1994-95 Telecom is opened to private mobile and basic operators (National Telecom Policy 1994; the first mobile call in India is made on 31 July 1995); private airlines (Jet, East-West, Damania) fly; private power producers are invited (the Enron Dabhol project becomes a famous controversy); India joins the World Trade Organisation (1995); the Internet becomes publicly available on 15 August 1995.

What was not reformed: labour laws, agriculture, land, the public sector itself (selling control of state companies was politically impossible), insurance (a bill failed in 1994), and the state governments' finances. These unfinished items are still on the agenda in 2026.

Results by 1996: growth recovered from 1.4% (1991-92) to 7.3% (1995-96); reserves rose from $1 billion to over $20 billion; exports grew about 20% a year; foreign direct investment went from almost nothing to over $2 billion a year; inflation fell from 14% to about 8%; the fiscal deficit fell from 8.4% to about 5% of GDP. Software exports crossed $1 billion; Infosys listed on the stock exchange (1993). Poverty by the official line fell to about 36% in 1993-94. The cost: the subsidies cut in 1991 hurt the poor; many public companies went sick; and the first stock-market scam of the new era, Harshad Mehta's diversion of about Rs 4,000 crore of bank money into shares (1992), showed that the regulators had not kept pace.

Ayodhya, 6 December 1992

  • 1991 The BJP wins Uttar Pradesh. The VHP announces that temple construction will begin. Rao sets up negotiations and asks the Supreme Court for help.
  • November 1992 The VHP announces a symbolic kar seva (volunteer work) at the site on 6 December. Chief Minister Kalyan Singh gives the Supreme Court a written assurance that the mosque will be protected. Intelligence reports warn Rao of a plan to demolish it. Central forces are sent to Faizabad but kept outside Ayodhya, waiting for the state government to ask for them. It never does.
  • 6 December 1992 Before an audience of BJP and VHP leaders, including Advani, Murli Manohar Joshi and Uma Bharti, about 150,000 volunteers break through the barriers and, in five hours, demolish the 464-year-old mosque with pickaxes and ropes. The police watch. A temporary temple is built on the rubble overnight. Rao, by most accounts, spent the afternoon unreachable; his defenders say he had no legal way to act without the state's request and feared a bloodbath if the army fired on the crowd.
  • December 1992 to January 1993 Riots across India kill about 2,000 people. In Bombay, two waves of violence (December and January) kill about 900, mostly Muslims, with the Shiv Sena and the police implicated (the Srikrishna Commission later documented it; almost no one was punished). The four BJP state governments are dismissed. The RSS, VHP and two Muslim organisations are banned for a time.
  • 12 March 1993 In revenge, 12 bombs planted by the Bombay underworld of Dawood Ibrahim, with Pakistani intelligence support, kill 257 people in Bombay in a single afternoon: the first mass-casualty terrorist attack on an Indian city, and the start of a new kind of threat.
  • 1993-94 The government acquires the land (Ayodhya Act, 1993) and asks the Supreme Court whether a temple had stood there; the court declines to answer. The legal case would take until 2019.

Politics and institutions

  • Panchayati Raj: the 73rd and 74th Amendments (passed December 1992, in force 1993) gave constitutional status to about 250,000 village councils and all city governments, with elections every five years and one-third of seats reserved for women. Over a million women entered elected office at once, the largest such step anywhere. It is the government's least-noticed and perhaps most lasting democratic achievement.
  • Mandal implemented: after the Supreme Court's 1992 ruling, OBC reservation in central jobs began in 1993 with the creamy-layer exclusion.
  • New watchdogs: the National Human Rights Commission (1993), the National Commission for Minorities (1992) and the National Commission for Women (1992).
  • The courts and the Election Commission pushed back against the executive: the Supreme Court's Bommai judgment (1994) ended the easy misuse of President's Rule, and its 1993 judgment created the collegium; Chief Election Commissioner T.N. Seshan enforced the model code of conduct, spending limits and voter identity cards so fiercely that politicians of all parties combined to dilute his powers (1993-95).
  • Survival at a price: in July 1993 the government won a no-confidence vote 265 to 251 after four Jharkhand Mukti Morcha MPs voted for it; they were later found to have received money. The Supreme Court ruled (1998) that MPs could not be prosecuted for bribes taken for votes in Parliament, a ruling it reversed in 2024. Rao was also charged in cases over a forged document (St Kitts) and a cheated British businessman (Lakhubhai Pathak); he was acquitted in all of them, some after his death.
  • The hawala scandal (1996): a diary of payments through an illegal money-transfer network named politicians of every party, including Advani (who resigned from Parliament until cleared) and several Congress ministers. All were eventually acquitted for lack of evidence, but the stench helped defeat Congress.
  • The states turned against Congress: it lost Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka (1994), Maharashtra and Gujarat (1995); in Uttar Pradesh the Dalit-based Bahujan Samaj Party's Mayawati became chief minister (1995) with BJP support, after a violent break with her ally Mulayam Singh Yadav. The era of regional parties had arrived.

Security and foreign policy

  • Punjab: after elections in February 1992 (boycotted by the Akalis), Chief Minister Beant Singh and police chief K.P.S. Gill crushed the insurgency by 1993 with methods (encounter killings, disappearances, bounties) that human rights groups documented and courts largely ignored. Beant Singh was assassinated in 1995, but Punjab stayed at peace.
  • Kashmir: the insurgency peaked (the Hazratbal shrine siege, 1993; the destruction of the Charar-e-Sharief shrine, 1995; foreign fighters replacing local ones). The government fought it, lost the international argument at the UN Human Rights Commission in 1994 by only a narrow margin (with Vajpayee leading India's delegation, a gesture of national unity), and finally held state elections in 1996 that brought Farooq Abdullah back.
  • Look East (1992): with the Soviet Union gone, Rao reoriented India towards Southeast Asia (ASEAN dialogue partnership), engaged the military regime in Myanmar, and visited Singapore, South Korea, Japan and China. Full diplomatic relations with Israel were established in January 1992. A 1993 agreement with China to keep "peace and tranquillity" on the border held until 2020.
  • Nuclear: he prepared a nuclear test at Pokhran in December 1995, which American satellites detected; under US pressure it was called off. He is reported to have told Vajpayee in 1996 that the bomb was ready ("saamagri tayyar hai").

Society and culture

  • The national midday meal scheme for schoolchildren (1995), the first national old-age pension (1995), a World Bank-funded District Primary Education Programme (1994), the ban on sex-selection tests (1994), the first National Family Health Survey (1992-93), and the tribal self-rule law for scheduled areas (PESA, 1996). The abolition of family-planning targets (1996) ended the Emergency's legacy of coercion.
  • Disasters: the Latur earthquake (1993, about 10,000 dead) and the Surat plague (1994), which caused worldwide panic and a flight of 300,000 people from the city.
  • Television transformed: Star TV (1991), Zee TV (1992), MTV, and cable operators in every lane ended Doordarshan's monopoly within three years. Satellite news, soap operas and music videos changed what Indians saw, wanted and argued about. Economic liberalisation and cultural globalisation arrived together: Coca-Cola returned (1993), Sushmita Sen and Aishwarya Rai won Miss Universe and Miss World in the same year (1994), and the big family films Hum Aapke Hain Koun (1994) and Dilwale Dulhania Le Jayenge (1995) sold a confident, consuming, traditional-but-modern India to itself and to the diaspora. A.R. Rahman's music (Roja, 1992; Bombay, 1995) and Mani Ratnam's films took the riots and the insurgency as subjects. Vikram Seth's A Suitable Boy (1993) became the longest novel in English in a generation.
  • In cricket, Sachin Tendulkar became the face of the new India; India co-hosted the 1996 World Cup. Viswanathan Anand challenged Garry Kasparov for the world chess title (1995).

The ups

  • The 1991 reforms: India's economic turning point, carried out without a majority and without a crisis of politics.
  • Default avoided; reserves from $1 billion to $20 billion; growth to 7%.
  • Panchayati Raj: a million women in office; local democracy constitutionally guaranteed.
  • Punjab pacified; Kashmir brought back to elections.
  • Look East, Israel, China, the WTO: a foreign policy for the post-Cold War world.
  • Telecom, Internet, cable TV, private banks and airlines: the infrastructure of the next decade.

The downs

  • 6 December 1992: a mosque destroyed under the state's eyes, 2,000 dead in riots, the Bombay blasts, and a permanent change in how Muslims saw their security in India.
  • The JMM bribery vote, the hawala revelations, the Sukh Ram telecom scandal (1996), and Harshad Mehta: the reform era began with a stink of money.
  • Punjab's peace bought with extrajudicial killings; Kashmir's human-rights record.
  • Reforms that stopped short: labour, agriculture, public-sector sales, state finances.
  • The Congress lost the south, west and north to regional parties under him, which his party blamed on him alone.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

  • Imposed President's Rule in Uttar Pradesh in November 1992, or moved central forces into Ayodhya, despite the legal and political risks. He had the intelligence, and the cabinet and the courts would likely have backed him afterwards. His inaction remains the biggest stain on his record.
  • Pushed reforms into the "second generation" (labour, agriculture, privatisation) in 1994-95 when growth was strong, rather than slowing down as elections neared.
  • Protected the poor better during the 1991 adjustment, which cut food and fertiliser subsidies and raised prices before the growth arrived.
  • Kept his hands clean of the JMM vote.

Could have been worse

  • India could have defaulted, taken an IMF programme in the harsh Latin American style, and had a lost decade. Instead it had its best decade since independence.
  • The reforms could have been reversed after the Babri riots, the Mehta scam, or the 1993-95 state election defeats, as many in Congress wanted. Rao held the line.
  • Firing on the Ayodhya crowd might have killed thousands and started a civil war; several officers believed so at the time.

What it chose

Economic revolution by stealth and political survival by any means. He spoke rarely, decided slowly, and when he acted on the economy he changed the country more than any prime minister since Nehru. On Ayodhya, he chose not to act, and that choice also changed the country.

UF

Vajpayee for 13 days, then Deve Gowda and Gujral: the United Front years

The 1996 election produced a Parliament nobody could run. The BJP tried for 13 days, then a 13-party "United Front" governed for 22 months with Congress's reluctant support, under two prime ministers few had expected. The economy got a famous budget and a slowdown; foreign policy got a doctrine; the nuclear bomb waited in its vault.

What they inherited

A growing economy (8% in 1996-97), reserves of $20 billion, reforms half-done, a Parliament split three ways, and a BJP that had shown it could reach the top but not yet stay there.

What the governments did

Vajpayee's 13 days

President Sharma invited the largest party. The BJP, still untouchable to most parties because of Ayodhya, found no partners. On 28 May 1996 Vajpayee gave a speech in the Lok Sabha that became famous ("Governments will come and go, parties will be made and unmade, but this country should survive, its democracy should survive"), announced his resignation, and left for the opposition benches. The episode made him, rather than Advani, the acceptable face of the BJP, and taught the party it needed allies. Both lessons paid off in 1998.

Deve Gowda: the farmer from Karnataka

  • A coalition of 13 parties agreed on a "Common Minimum Programme" that kept the Rao reforms going while promising more for farmers and the poor.
  • The "Dream Budget" (28 February 1997): Chidambaram cut the top income tax rate to 30%, corporate tax to 35%, and customs duties, and offered an amnesty for undeclared money (the Voluntary Disclosure of Income Scheme, which brought in Rs 33,000 crore). Tax collections rose as rates fell, a lesson all later finance ministers kept.
  • The Ganga Water Treaty with Bangladesh (December 1996) settled a 20-year dispute over the Farakka barrage; the Mahakali treaty with Nepal (1996) followed Rao's groundwork.
  • The nuclear option was kept: India refused to sign the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty (1996), with the diplomat Arundhati Ghose famously blocking it at Geneva. Gowda considered a test and decided against it.
  • The Women's Reservation Bill (one-third of Lok Sabha seats) was introduced in September 1996 and blocked by the government's own OBC allies; it would take 27 years to pass. Jammu and Kashmir's state election (September 1996) returned Farooq Abdullah after nine years of central rule. The Telecom Regulatory Authority (1997) was set up.
  • Gowda was a hard-working but unglamorous leader who fell asleep at public events and spoke little Hindi; Congress's new president Kesri, who wanted the job himself, withdrew support in March 1997 for no stated reason. Gowda lost the confidence vote 158 to 292.

Gujral: a doctrine and a dignified exit

  • The Gujral Doctrine, formulated while he was foreign minister: India, as the big neighbour, should give more than it gets from Bangladesh, Bhutan, the Maldives, Nepal and Sri Lanka, should not interfere in their internal affairs, and should settle disputes bilaterally and peacefully. It shaped India's "neighbourhood first" approach, and was criticised for naivety when he reportedly closed down intelligence operations inside Pakistan.
  • The 50th anniversary of independence (15 August 1997) was marked with a midnight session of Parliament. K.R. Narayanan became the first Dalit President (July 1997).
  • The Fifth Pay Commission award (1997) raised government salaries steeply and wrecked state finances for a decade; the Asian financial crisis (1997) hit the region but India, with capital controls, was barely touched; growth slowed to 4.3% in 1997-98.
  • The fodder scam in Bihar (over Rs 900 crore stolen through fake bills for cattle feed) led to the arrest of Chief Minister Lalu Prasad Yadav (July 1997), who installed his wife Rabri Devi as chief minister. Mother Teresa died in Calcutta (September 1997). Arundhati Roy won the Booker Prize for The God of Small Things (October 1997).
  • The fall: the Jain Commission's interim report on Rajiv Gandhi's assassination (November 1997) accused the DMK, a coalition partner, of having helped the Tamil Tigers in Tamil Nadu. Congress demanded the DMK's expulsion; Gujral refused to abandon an ally on an interim report; Congress withdrew support; Parliament was dissolved on 4 December 1997.

The ups

  • Reforms survived a change of government, proving they were now national policy, not one party's.
  • The Dream Budget set tax rates that lasted; treaties with Bangladesh and Nepal; Kashmir's return to elected government.
  • The CTBT stand and the Gujral Doctrine: a confident, independent foreign policy from a weak government.
  • Vajpayee's 13 days made the BJP a possible party of government.

The downs

  • Two governments brought down by one Congress president's whims, with no policy reason.
  • The Pay Commission's cost; growth slowing; the second generation of reforms stalled.
  • Women's reservation blocked by the government's own allies.
  • Instability itself: four prime ministers in two years, three general elections in three years.

Could have done better

Had the CPI(M) allowed Jyoti Basu to become prime minister, the United Front would have had a leader of national stature and might have lasted. Had Congress behaved like a responsible supporter instead of a jealous one, the reforms and the budget might have been built on rather than interrupted.

Could have been worse

Coalitions of this kind collapsed into chaos in many countries. India's held together enough to pass a budget, sign treaties and hold elections on time, and the economy kept growing.

What it chose

Continuity in economics, caution on the bomb, generosity to neighbours, and survival for as long as Congress allowed.

What changed after

The 1998 election brought the BJP to power with allies, and within two months the bomb that Rao, Gowda and Gujral had kept in the vault was tested. The lesson of 1996-98, that a stable government needs a big party at its core, pushed voters towards the two national parties for the next twenty years.

ABV

Government 12 · 19 March 1998 to 22 May 2004

Atal Bihari Vajpayee: the bomb, the bus, Kargil, and the roads

The first non-Congress prime minister to complete a full term, Vajpayee made India an open nuclear power, fought and won a limited war with Pakistan, then spent his last two years trying to make peace with it. His government built the highways, opened telecom, and ran a coalition of two dozen parties with a light touch. It also presided over the Gujarat riots of 2002 and then lost an election it was sure it had won.

Party
Bharatiya Janata Party, leading the National Democratic Alliance (NDA)
Type of government
Coalition. In 1998 the BJP had 182 seats and depended on about 13 allies, including Jayalalithaa's AIADMK, which withdrew in April 1999; the government lost a confidence vote by one vote (269 to 270) and governed as caretaker through the Kargil war. In the September-October 1999 election the BJP again won 182 seats but the NDA won about 300, with 24 parties, and lasted the full term.

What he inherited

A slowing economy (4.3% in 1997-98) hit by the Asian crisis, reforms stuck in their "second generation", a nuclear weapon built but untested, Kashmir still bleeding, a Pakistan that had its own untested bomb, a Congress in disarray, and the first serious chance for the Hindu nationalist movement to show it could govern. Also a coalition in which the prime minister's own party held less than a third of the House.

The conditions he worked under

  • International sanctions after the nuclear tests, then the opposite: the 9/11 attacks (2001) turned the United States into a partner against Islamist terrorism and made Pakistan both an American ally and a source of the terrorism India faced.
  • The world economy slowed in 2001-02 (the dot-com bust), then entered a global boom from 2003 that India rode. Oil was cheap for most of the term.
  • Coalition arithmetic meant the BJP's core agenda (the Ram temple, the Uniform Civil Code, Article 370) was formally shelved in the NDA's common programme. The RSS and VHP pushed from outside; the allies pushed from inside.
  • Weather and disasters: the Odisha super-cyclone (1999, about 10,000 dead), the Bhuj earthquake in Gujarat (26 January 2001, about 20,000 dead), and a severe drought in 2002-03.

What the government did

The bomb and Pakistan: six years of whiplash

  • 11 and 13 May 1998 Five nuclear tests at Pokhran ("Operation Shakti"), two months into the government. India declares itself a nuclear weapons state and adopts a doctrine of No First Use and a minimum deterrent. The United States, Japan and others impose sanctions; Pakistan tests two weeks later. The sanctions fade within two years; the Jaswant Singh-Strobe Talbott talks (14 rounds) open the most serious dialogue India and the United States had ever had.
  • February 1999 Vajpayee rides a bus to Lahore and signs the Lahore Declaration with Nawaz Sharif: two nuclear powers promising restraint and talks. He visits the Minar-e-Pakistan to show India accepts Pakistan's existence.
  • May to July 1999 Kargil: Pakistani soldiers disguised as militants, on the orders of army chief Pervez Musharraf, have occupied Indian posts along 150 km of the Line of Control in Ladakh during the winter. India, caught off guard, retakes them in two months of fighting at 15,000 to 18,000 feet, using air power but refusing to cross the Line of Control. 527 Indian soldiers die. The United States, for the first time, sides openly with India and forces Pakistan to withdraw. Televised live, Kargil is the first war most Indians watched. Musharraf seizes power in Pakistan in October.
  • 24-31 December 1999 Indian Airlines flight IC-814 is hijacked to Kandahar, Afghanistan, under the Taliban. After a week, India releases three jailed militants, including Masood Azhar, who founds Jaish-e-Mohammed; Jaswant Singh personally escorts them to Kandahar. The decision haunted the government and the country: Azhar's group later attacked Parliament (2001) and Pulwama (2019).
  • July 2001 The Agra summit with Musharraf fails over the wording of a joint statement on Kashmir and terrorism.
  • 13 December 2001 Five terrorists attack the Parliament building; nine Indians die, the attackers are killed before reaching the chambers. India mobilises its entire army for ten months (Operation Parakram), the largest deployment since 1971; war is avoided after American pressure on Pakistan and a vague Musharraf promise (January 2002) to curb terrorism. The mobilisation costs about 800 soldiers' lives in accidents and mine-laying and shows that India had no plan for a quick, limited war; it leads to the "Cold Start" doctrine.
  • April 2003 to January 2004 Vajpayee extends a "hand of friendship" from Srinagar; a ceasefire on the Line of Control takes effect in November 2003 and holds for years; at the Islamabad summit in January 2004 Musharraf promises not to let Pakistani territory be used for terrorism and a "composite dialogue" begins. On Kashmir, Vajpayee's formula is "insaniyat, jamhooriyat, Kashmiriyat" (humanity, democracy, Kashmir's own culture). The 2002 state election in Kashmir, unusually fair, produces a new government.

The economy and infrastructure: the quiet revolution

In simple words

Vajpayee's government did not announce a big-bang reform like 1991. It did something that mattered as much in daily life: it built roads and made phones cheap. The highways programme connected the four big cities; the village roads programme reached tens of thousands of villages; and a change in telecom rules took India from 1 million mobile phones to over 30 million in five years. It also sold government companies to private buyers, something no earlier government had dared.

  • The Golden Quadrilateral (launched 1999-2001): 5,846 km of four- and six-lane highway linking Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata, India's largest infrastructure project since the railways, plus the North-South and East-West corridors. Most of it was finished by 2012.
  • Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana (December 2000): all-weather roads to villages; over 700,000 km have been built since.
  • New Telecom Policy (1999): mobile operators, crushed by huge fixed licence fees, were allowed to pay a share of revenue instead. Prices collapsed, and the mobile revolution began. Internet was opened to private providers; the Information Technology Act (2000) gave legal status to electronic documents; software and back-office exports went from about $3 billion to $13 billion.
  • Disinvestment: Arun Shourie sold government control of BALCO (aluminium), VSNL (international telecom, to Tata), IPCL (petrochemicals, to Reliance), Hindustan Zinc, Maruti (to Suzuki), Modern Foods, CMC and several hotels. Attempts to sell the oil companies BPCL and HPCL were stopped by the Supreme Court and by the government's own ministers.
  • Rules for the future: the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act (2003) set legal limits on deficits; the Electricity Act (2003) opened power generation and trading; insurance was opened to private and foreign companies (1999); a new pension system for government employees (2004) replaced guaranteed pensions with market-linked accounts; Special Economic Zones were introduced (2000); the Kisan Credit Card (1998) gave farmers cheap loans; Antyodaya Anna Yojana (2000) gave the poorest families grain at Rs 2-3 a kilo.
  • Numbers: growth averaged about 6% a year (3.9% in the drought year 2002-03, then 8% in 2003-04); inflation fell to 4-5%; interest rates were cut sharply, starting the housing-loan boom; foreign reserves crossed $100 billion (December 2003); the stock market began a five-year bull run. The fiscal deficit stayed high at 5-6% of GDP.
  • Scandals: the Tehelka sting (2001) filmed the BJP president and a defence official taking money from fake arms dealers; George Fernandes resigned and was reinstated. The Ketan Parekh stock scam and the collapse of the UTI's US-64 fund (2001) hurt small investors. The "coffin scam" allegation over Kargil purchases was never proven.

Politics and institutions

  • Coalition dharma: Vajpayee managed 24 allies with patience, concessions and personal charm. Three new states were created in November 2000 without violence: Chhattisgarh, Uttaranchal (now Uttarakhand) and Jharkhand. A commission to review the Constitution (2000-02) produced many recommendations and few changes.
  • Anti-terror law: the Prevention of Terrorism Act (2002) was passed at a joint session after the Rajya Sabha rejected it; it was widely misused against minorities and political opponents in some states and was repealed in 2004.
  • Education: Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan (2001) aimed at every child in school; the 86th Amendment (2002) made education a Fundamental Right for children aged 6 to 14; the midday meal scheme was extended by Supreme Court order (2001). But the NCERT textbook rewriting under HRD minister Joshi was attacked as "saffronisation" of history, and the new Indian Institutes of Technology and Management were pressured on fees and autonomy.
  • Gujarat, 2002: on 27 February 2002, 59 Hindu pilgrims returning from Ayodhya died when a coach of the Sabarmati Express was set on fire at Godhra station. Over the following days, mobs across Gujarat killed about 1,000 people by the official count (790 Muslims and 254 Hindus; other estimates reach 2,000), with rape, arson and the police often absent or complicit; 150,000 people were displaced. Chief Minister Narendra Modi's government was accused of allowing the violence; courts later convicted over a hundred people for specific massacres (Naroda Patiya, Gulberg Society, the Bilkis Bano case) while a Supreme Court-appointed team found no evidence to prosecute Modi himself (2012, upheld 2022). Vajpayee publicly reminded Modi of "raj dharma", the ruler's duty to treat all equally, and reportedly wanted him removed; the party, at its Goa meeting in April 2002, kept him. Modi won the December 2002 state election in a landslide.
  • Religion otherwise: the Australian missionary Graham Staines and his two sons were burned alive in Odisha (January 1999) by a Bajrang Dal activist; attacks on Christians in the Dangs district of Gujarat (1998) drew Vajpayee's call for a "national debate on conversions", which alarmed minorities. On Ayodhya he spoke of "an unfinished national sentiment" (December 2000) but kept the issue in the courts and in talks mediated by religious leaders; the temple was not touched in his term.

Foreign policy beyond Pakistan

  • The United States: President Clinton's five-day visit in March 2000 (the first by an American president in 22 years) and Vajpayee's description of the two countries as "natural allies" marked the end of the Cold War estrangement. After 9/11 India offered bases and intelligence. The "Next Steps in Strategic Partnership" (January 2004) on nuclear, space and high-technology trade led to the 2005 nuclear deal. India refused, however, to send troops to Iraq in 2003.
  • China: Vajpayee's 2003 visit produced a formula: India accepted Tibet as part of China in writing; China accepted trade through Sikkim, implying it was Indian. Special representatives were appointed for the border talks.
  • Neighbours: Bhutan's army cleared Indian insurgent camps at India's request (2003); relations with Nepal's monarchy and Bangladesh were steady; Israel's prime minister Sharon visited (2003) as defence ties grew.

Society, science and culture

  • The Delhi Metro's first line opened (December 2002), the beginning of a national metro boom. The Konkan Railway and the first private airports' planning fell in this era. The pulse-polio campaign cut cases to a few hundred a year.
  • Information technology became India's calling card: Infosys listed on Nasdaq (1999), Wipro on the New York Stock Exchange (2000), Bangalore and Hyderabad ("Cyberabad", under Chandrababu Naidu) became global brands, and the Y2K fix and call-centre boom created the first mass white-collar jobs for the new middle class.
  • Culture went global and glossy: Kaun Banega Crorepati (2000) made Amitabh Bachchan a television emperor; Lagaan (2001) was nominated for an Oscar; Dil Chahta Hai (2001) spoke for urban youth; Bollywood found the diaspora market. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam, the "missile man", became a hugely popular President (2002). Kalpana Chawla, the first Indian-born woman in space, died in the Columbia disaster (2003). Karnam Malleswari became the first Indian woman to win an Olympic medal (2000). India reached the cricket World Cup final (2003) and the Agni-II missile and the first GSLV rocket (2001) flew.

The ups

  • The nuclear tests, managed so that sanctions were gone within two years and the United States became a partner.
  • Kargil won without crossing the Line of Control, and world opinion won with it.
  • Highways, village roads, cheap mobile phones, the Delhi Metro, the IT boom: the physical and digital base of the 2000s.
  • Privatisation begun; fiscal rules, the Electricity Act, insurance opening, the pension reform.
  • The first stable, full-term coalition, and three new states without bloodshed.
  • Peace moves with Pakistan from 2003 that produced the longest ceasefire in decades.
  • Education as a fundamental right; Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan.

The downs

  • Gujarat 2002, and the decision to keep the chief minister in office.
  • Kandahar: releasing Masood Azhar.
  • Kargil was an intelligence failure before it was a victory; Operation Parakram was a ten-month bluff that cost hundreds of lives.
  • POTA's misuse; the textbook controversy; the Staines murder and the "debate on conversions".
  • Tehelka and the stock scams; disinvestment stalled by its own ministers.
  • Fiscal deficits stayed high; rural distress and farmer suicides (especially in Andhra Pradesh and Maharashtra) grew while "India Shining" was advertised.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

  • Removed Modi in March 2002, as Vajpayee himself apparently wished, or at least imposed central rule in Gujarat until the violence stopped. The party chose electoral advantage; the stain lasted.
  • Refused the Kandahar swap or negotiated harder; most security analysts now think the three released men did more damage than the hijackers could have.
  • Used the 2003-04 boom to push harder on privatisation, labour and agriculture rather than an early election and an advertising campaign.
  • Acted on rural distress that the "India Shining" campaign ignored, which cost the election.
  • Fixed intelligence after Kargil faster; the review committee's reforms took years.

Could have been worse

  • Kargil could have escalated across the Line of Control into a nuclear-shadowed general war; restraint was a real choice and a costly one.
  • December 2001 could have become war with Pakistan; it did not.
  • The Ram temple could have been built by force in 2002-03, as the VHP demanded; the government held the line in the courts.
  • The nuclear tests could have left India isolated for a decade, as 1974 did. Diplomacy prevented that.

What it chose

Nuclear openness with diplomatic patience; coalition compromise over ideological purity; infrastructure and telecom over headline reforms; restraint in war; and, in Gujarat, party over principle.

The highways, telecom and fiscal framework powered the 9% growth of 2005-08 under his successor, who kept most of his policies and signed the nuclear deal his talks had begun. MMS

Government 13 · 22 May 2004 to 26 May 2014

Manmohan Singh: the boom, the rights, the scams, and the drift

The economist who had written the 1991 reforms became an unelected prime minister by the choice of Sonia Gandhi, who declined the job herself. For five years India grew faster than it ever had, lifted more people out of poverty than ever before, and gave citizens new legal rights to information, work, food and schooling. The second five years brought scandal, inflation, a policy standstill and the worst defeat in Congress's history.

Indian National Congress, leading the United Progressive Alliance (UPA)

What he inherited

An economy accelerating (8% in 2003-04), reserves above $110 billion, low inflation, falling interest rates, new highways and cheap phones, a ceasefire with Pakistan, a strategic partnership with the United States begun, and a legal commitment to cut deficits. Also: a mandate that voters had given against "India Shining", meaning they wanted growth to reach the villages; and a party whose leader had made him prime minister and could unmake him.

The conditions he worked under

  • The greatest global boom of modern times (2003-08), with cheap money, booming trade, China's rise, and foreign investors pouring into "emerging markets". Then the global financial crisis of 2008, the worst since 1929, and a slow, unstable world economy afterwards: the European debt crisis (2010-12) and the "taper tantrum" (2013), when American interest-rate signals pulled money out of countries like India.
  • Oil prices went from $35 a barrel to $147 (2008), fell, then stayed above $100 for most of 2011-14, hurting India's import bill and subsidy budget.
  • Two power centres: the prime minister ran the government; the party president ran the politics and, through the National Advisory Council, much of the social agenda. Allies ran key ministries as their own domains ("coalition compulsions", as the prime minister called it).
  • Terror from Pakistan, Maoist insurgency in the heartland, and a new media environment: 24-hour news channels, mobile phones in every hand, and from 2009 social media.

What the government did

The economy, part one: the boom (2004-08)

In simple words

For three years in a row (2005-08) India grew above 9% a year, as fast as China. Companies invested in factories, ports and power plants; foreign money flowed in; the stock market quadrupled; and tax collections rose so fast that the government could pay for huge welfare programmes and still cut the deficit. It felt, for a while, like the long-promised take-off.

  • Growth averaged 8.4% a year in UPA-I. Investment reached 38% of GDP, a record. Tax revenues doubled in four years. The fiscal deficit fell to about 2.5% of GDP in 2007-08, the lowest in decades. Reserves passed $300 billion (2008).
  • States adopted a value-added tax (2005), the first step towards GST, which the government proposed in 2006 but could not get the states or the BJP to agree to. Special Economic Zones were given a law (2005), prompting land fights. Low-cost airlines (IndiGo, SpiceJet) made flying middle-class. Mobile subscribers went from 33 million (2004) to over 900 million (2014); the IT and back-office industry's exports grew from about $13 billion to about $86 billion.
  • The 2008 budget waived about Rs 60,000-70,000 crore of farmers' loans; the Sixth Pay Commission (2008) raised government wages sharply. When the global crisis struck in late 2008, the government spent its way through it with tax cuts and extra spending; growth dipped to 6.7% and recovered to 8.6%. India was praised worldwide for weathering the storm, but the stimulus was never withdrawn, and inflation and deficits followed.

The rights revolution: laws that changed daily life

  • Right to Information Act (2005): any citizen can ask any public authority for its records for Rs 10. It exposed corruption from village ration shops to the Commonwealth Games, and became a model copied abroad (and, activists say, has been weakened by later governments).
  • National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (2005, launched 2006, later named after Mahatma Gandhi, MGNREGA): a legal right to 100 days of paid manual work a year for every rural household. At its peak it reached over 50 million households a year, raised rural wages, and gave women (about half the workers) their own earnings. Critics called it a "monument to failure" that paid people to dig ditches; the next government kept and expanded it.
  • Forest Rights Act (2006): recognised the land rights of forest-dwelling tribal communities. Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act (2009, in force 2010): every child aged 6-14 must get a school place, and private schools must reserve a quarter of their seats for poor children. National Food Security Act (2013): cheap grain as a legal right for two-thirds of the population. Land Acquisition Act (2013): consent of most affected families and compensation at two to four times market value before land is taken for projects. Plus the Domestic Violence Act (2005), the Street Vendors Act (2014), the Sexual Harassment at Workplace Act (2013), and the Lokpal Act (2013) creating a national anti-corruption ombudsman.
  • Health and welfare: the National Rural Health Mission (2005) put a trained health worker (ASHA) in nearly every village and paid women to deliver in hospitals (institutional births rose from under 40% to about 80%); India recorded its last polio case in 2011 and was declared polio-free in 2014. The urban renewal mission (JNNURM) funded buses, water and sewerage in 65 cities.
  • Aadhaar (2009): the biometric identity number, run by Infosys co-founder Nandan Nilekani, enrolled 600 million people by 2014 and allowed Direct Benefit Transfer of subsidies to start (2013). The BJP opposed it then, adopted it in 2014, and built its welfare delivery on it.
  • Reservation extended: 27% of seats in central universities and IITs/IIMs for OBCs (2006), after protests by upper-caste students; new IITs, IIMs and central universities were opened (2008-09) partly to expand the total seats.

The nuclear deal and foreign policy

  • The India-US Civil Nuclear Agreement: announced with President Bush in July 2005, it ended India's 30-year isolation from nuclear trade in exchange for separating civilian and military reactors and accepting international inspection of the civilian ones. The Left parties, who saw it as subordination to America, withdrew support in July 2008; the government won a confidence vote 275-256 amid an ugly cash-for-votes scandal (BJP MPs displayed bundles of currency in the House). The Nuclear Suppliers Group granted India a unique waiver in September 2008. The deal's practical fruit (new reactors) was small because of a tough Indian liability law (2010), but its strategic effect was large: the United States had accepted India as a nuclear power, and the relationship deepened in defence, trade and technology.
  • Pakistan: the composite dialogue and a secret back-channel (2004-07) reportedly came close to a Kashmir settlement based on open borders, self-governance and joint mechanisms without changing the map. Musharraf's domestic collapse (2007) ended it. Then the Mumbai attacks of 26-29 November 2008: ten Lashkar-e-Taiba gunmen from Pakistan killed 166 people at the Taj and Oberoi hotels, the railway station and a Jewish centre, on live television for 60 hours. The government chose not to retaliate militarily, a decision praised for restraint and attacked for weakness. Ajmal Kasab, the one gunman captured, was hanged in 2012. Talks resumed in fits and starts; the 2009 Sharm el-Sheikh statement, which mentioned Pakistani concerns about Balochistan, was condemned at home.
  • China: trade boomed (to $65 billion, with a large deficit), the border stayed mostly quiet under 2005 and 2013 agreements, but incidents grew: stapled visas for Kashmiris, claims on Arunachal Pradesh, and a three-week intrusion at Depsang in Ladakh (2013).
  • Neighbours and the world: Sri Lanka's civil war ended in 2009 with heavy Tamil civilian deaths that India was accused of enabling; Bangladesh under Sheikh Hasina (from 2009) became a security partner and a land-boundary agreement was signed (2011, ratified 2015); the Teesta water deal was blocked by West Bengal's chief minister (2011); Nepal abolished its monarchy (2008) with Indian encouragement; Afghanistan got an Indian-built parliament and highway. India joined the G20 as a founding member of its leaders' summits (2008) and BRICS (2009), and bargained hard at the Copenhagen climate summit (2009). The National Action Plan on Climate Change (2008) and the National Solar Mission (2010) began India's renewable push.

Security at home

  • Terrorism: bombings in Delhi (2005), Varanasi (2006), Mumbai's trains (July 2006, 209 dead), Hyderabad (2007), Jaipur, Ahmedabad and Delhi (2008), Pune (2010), Mumbai (2011) and Hyderabad (2013), many by the home-grown Indian Mujahideen with Pakistani backing. After 26/11 a National Investigation Agency was created and commando units were spread across cities. The Samjhauta Express (2007) and Malegaon (2008) bombings, in which Hindu extremist suspects were charged, produced a bitter argument about "saffron terror"; in 2019 and 2025 the courts acquitted all the accused for lack of evidence.
  • The Maoists: the prime minister called them "the single biggest internal security challenge" (2006). The Chhattisgarh government armed a tribal militia, Salwa Judum (2005-11), which the Supreme Court declared illegal. In April 2010 Maoists killed 76 paramilitary soldiers in one ambush at Dantewada; in May 2013 they wiped out the Chhattisgarh Congress leadership at Jhiram Ghati. Violence peaked around 2010 and then began a long decline as roads, forces and development reached the forest districts.
  • Kashmir: a dispute over land for the Amarnath pilgrimage split Jammu and the valley (2008); a summer of stone-throwing protests in 2010 killed over 110 young people in police firing; a panel of interlocutors recommended autonomy measures (2011-12) that were ignored; Afzal Guru's hanging (2013) for the Parliament attack revived anger. Elections in 2008 and 2014 drew large turnouts.
  • The Northeast: Assam saw violence between Bodos and Bengali Muslims (2012, over 70 dead, 400,000 displaced); Manipur's Irom Sharmila continued her 16-year hunger strike against the AFSPA; a committee recommended repealing the Act (2005) and the government did not act.

The economy, part two: inflation, scandal and paralysis (2009-14)

  • Inflation averaged about 10% a year from 2009 to 2013, driven by food prices, fuel, the stimulus never withdrawn, and rural wages rising faster than productivity. For the poor it wiped out much of the gain from welfare; for the middle class it defined the government.
  • The scandals: in 2010 the Comptroller and Auditor General said the 2008 allocation of 2G telecom licences at 2001 prices had caused a "presumptive loss" of up to Rs 1.76 lakh crore; Telecom Minister A. Raja was jailed for 15 months awaiting trial, and the Supreme Court cancelled 122 licences (2012). In 2017 the trial court acquitted everyone, saying the prosecution had proved nothing. The Commonwealth Games (2010) cost about Rs 70,000 crore amid corruption charges against its organiser. "Coalgate" (2012): the CAG estimated Rs 1.86 lakh crore of gains to companies given coal blocks without auction, some while the prime minister held the coal portfolio; the Supreme Court cancelled 214 blocks (2014) and some officials and businessmen were later convicted. Add the Adarsh housing scam, a helicopter deal with AgustaWestland, a railway bribery case, and a land case involving Sonia Gandhi's son-in-law, and the government's reputation collapsed, regardless of what courts eventually found.
  • Anna Hazare and the anti-corruption movement (2011): the 74-year-old Gandhian's fasts in Delhi for a strong Lokpal drew huge urban crowds and nonstop television. The movement produced the Lokpal Act (2013), the Aam Aadmi Party of Arvind Kejriwal (which won Delhi in 2013 and again in 2015), and a mood of disgust that Narendra Modi rode in 2014.
  • Policy paralysis: frightened officials stopped signing files; environmental clearances, land and coal shortages stalled hundreds of projects; the 2012 budget's retrospective tax on Vodafone's 2007 purchase of an Indian company scared foreign investors; a nationwide blackout in July 2012 left 620 million people without power for two days. Growth fell to about 5-6%; the current account deficit hit 4.8% of GDP (2012-13); the rupee crashed from 44 to 68 per dollar (2011-13); India was labelled one of the "fragile five" economies. Banks, which had lent freely to power, steel and infrastructure firms in the boom, found their loans going bad: the "twin balance sheet" problem that would take the next government a decade to clean up.
  • The late rally: in 2012-14 Finance Minister Chidambaram cut the deficit, allowed foreign supermarkets (which cost the government its Trinamool ally), raised diesel prices gradually, and Raghuram Rajan at the RBI stabilised the rupee. Growth was recovering by 2014, but too late to matter politically.

Society and culture

  • Poverty fell faster than ever: by the official Tendulkar line, from 37% (2004-05) to 22% (2011-12), about 140 million people in seven years; rural wages rose; mobile phones, motorcycles and television reached the poor. Literacy reached 74% (2011); infant mortality fell from 58 to 40 per 1,000; the fertility rate fell to about 2.4 children per woman.
  • Women: the gang-rape and murder of a physiotherapy student on a Delhi bus (December 2012, "Nirbhaya") brought the largest spontaneous protests in a generation and a sweeping new criminal law (2013) with harsher punishments and new offences. The Women's Reservation Bill passed the Rajya Sabha (2010) and died in the Lok Sabha. The 2011 census showed a child sex ratio of 914 girls per 1,000 boys, the worst ever, despite the ban on sex selection.
  • Caste and religion: the Sachar Committee (2006) found Indian Muslims worse off than Dalits on many measures, with tiny shares in government jobs and bank credit; a 2007 commission recommended a Muslim quota that was never enacted. Anti-Christian violence in Odisha's Kandhamal (2008) killed dozens and displaced tens of thousands. The Muzaffarnagar riots (September 2013), which killed over 60 and displaced 50,000 in western Uttar Pradesh, poisoned politics before the 2014 election. The Delhi High Court decriminalised homosexuality (2009) and the Supreme Court reversed it (2013).
  • Federal change: Telangana was carved out of Andhra Pradesh (law passed in February 2014, state formed June 2014) after a decade of agitation; Darjeeling got an autonomous Gorkhaland council (2012).
  • Science: Chandrayaan-1 (2008) found water molecules on the Moon; the Mars Orbiter Mission was launched (November 2013) and reached Mars in September 2014 at a tenth of NASA's cost; the Agni-V long-range missile (2012), the nuclear submarine Arihant (launched 2009) and the carrier Vikramaditya (2013) arrived; India's own cryogenic rocket engine finally worked (January 2014).
  • Culture and sport: the Indian Premier League (2008) turned cricket into a billion-dollar entertainment business; India won the Twenty20 World Cup (2007) and the World Cup (2011) at home; Abhinav Bindra won India's first individual Olympic gold (2008) and India won six medals at London 2012; the Commonwealth Games in Delhi (2010) were a sporting success and an organisational scandal. Slumdog Millionaire and A.R. Rahman's two Oscars (2009), Rang De Basanti (2006), 3 Idiots (2009) and the multiplex boom; Kiran Desai (2006) and Aravind Adiga (2008) won Bookers; Facebook, YouTube and Twitter arrived in Indian politics; and in 2012 Aamir Khan's Satyamev Jayate brought social issues to Sunday television.

The ups

  • The fastest growth in India's history (2005-08) and the fastest fall in poverty (2005-12).
  • Rights-based laws: information, work, education, food, forests, land; the rural health mission; polio eliminated.
  • The nuclear deal and a new strategic relationship with the United States; India in the G20 and BRICS.
  • Aadhaar, direct transfers and the mobile explosion: the platform the next government built on.
  • Restraint after Mumbai 2008 that kept the world on India's side; Telangana created peacefully.
  • Chandrayaan, Mars, Agni-V, the 2011 World Cup.

The downs

  • Scandals on a scale that made the word "scam" the decade's defining word, whatever the courts later found.
  • Five years of 10% inflation.
  • Policy paralysis, stalled projects, bad loans, a crashing rupee, "fragile five".
  • Dual power centres: a prime minister who did not control his government's politics, and an "accidental" image he never escaped.
  • Mumbai 26/11 and the terror wave of 2005-08; Dantewada; the 2010 Kashmir summer; Kandhamal and Muzaffarnagar.
  • Reforms the government itself wanted (GST, pensions, insurance, labour) left for the next one.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

  • Withdrawn the 2008 stimulus by 2010 and kept the deficit and inflation down; the boom years' discipline was thrown away.
  • Auctioned spectrum and coal from the start, as officials recommended; the discretionary allocations that produced the scandals were a choice.
  • Acted on the scandals early: sacking Raja in 2008 rather than 2010, and not defending allies' ministries as untouchable.
  • Passed GST in 2010-11 by offering the states what they were eventually given in 2016.
  • Used the 2009 majority, larger than 2004's, for the second-generation reforms instead of treating it as a mandate only for welfare.
  • Made the prime minister the real head of government, or let him go in 2009 when he wished to.

Could have been worse

  • India could have had a 2008-style crisis itself; cautious banking rules kept it out of the worst, and growth never fell below 5%.
  • Mumbai 2008 could have become a war between two nuclear states.
  • The welfare laws could have been mere promises; by making them legal rights, the government made them hard for any successor to remove, and none has.
  • The Left could have blocked the nuclear deal; the government risked its survival and won.

What it chose

Growth with redistribution in the first term, and survival with drift in the second. It chose rights over reforms, allies' comfort over clean government, restraint over retaliation, and the United States over the Left. Manmohan Singh said in his last press conference that history would be kinder to him than the media; on poverty and on foreign policy it has been, on governance it has not.

Narendra Modi: welfare by pipeline, Hindu nationalism by law, and a bigger India in the world

The first prime minister born after independence, and the first since 1984 to win a majority on his own, Narendra Modi has governed for twelve years with a style unlike any predecessor: centralised, campaign-driven, delivered through digital pipes, and openly rooted in Hindu nationalism. India has become the world's fourth-largest economy, built infrastructure at a pace not seen before, and cut extreme poverty to a twentieth of its 2011 level. It has also seen a currency ban, a brutal pandemic, a border clash with China, a deep argument about the place of Muslims, and a contest over the independence of its institutions. This chapter is the hardest to write, because it is still being written.

What he inherited

An economy growing about 6% with inflation near 9%, a rupee that had just crashed, banks hiding a mountain of bad loans, hundreds of stalled projects, a fiscal deficit of 4.5%, reserves of $300 billion, and a welfare and identity architecture (Aadhaar, MGNREGA, food security, rural health) that worked but leaked. Also: the biggest mandate in thirty years, a demoralised opposition, a hopeful business class, a young population with mobile phones, and an electorate that had voted for "development" and "a strong leader" after a decade of coalition drift. And, from his own record in Gujarat, both a reputation for delivery and the shadow of 2002.

The conditions he worked under

  • Cheap oil (2014-16) was a windfall worth tens of billions of dollars a year; the government kept the savings as fuel taxes rather than passing them on, which funded roads and welfare.
  • A hostile neighbourhood that got worse: Pakistan's proxies attacked repeatedly (2016, 2019, 2025); China became openly aggressive (Doklam 2017, Galwan 2020); Bangladesh's friendly government fell (2024); Nepal, Sri Lanka and the Maldives swung between India and China.
  • The COVID-19 pandemic (2020-22): the deepest economic contraction since independence, and the deadliest event since Partition.
  • A fractured world: Trump's trade wars (from 2018), the Russia-Ukraine war (from 2022), the US-China rivalry, the Israel-Gaza war (from 2023), and in 2026 a US-Israel war with Iran that closed the Strait of Hormuz and produced what S&P Global called the largest energy supply disruption since the Second World War. India, importing over 85% of its oil and about 60% of its cooking gas, has been exposed to every one of these.
  • Politically, a near-absence of effective opposition from 2014 to 2024, and then, after the 2024 result, a dependence on allies and a revived opposition.

What the government did

Welfare by pipeline: the "JAM" state

In simple words

Earlier governments passed welfare laws and left delivery to a leaky bureaucracy. This government's bet was that if every family had a bank account (Jan Dhan), an identity number (Aadhaar) and a mobile phone, the state could send money and track benefits directly, cutting out middlemen. It then used this pipe to deliver physical goods on a scale no earlier government attempted: toilets, gas cylinders, electricity connections, houses, tap water, health insurance.

  • Jan Dhan Yojana (2014): over 560 million bank accounts opened, most for people who had never had one. Direct Benefit Transfer now sends more than Rs 40 lakh crore (cumulatively) of pensions, scholarships, fuel subsidies and farm payments straight to accounts; the government claims savings of over Rs 3 lakh crore from removing fake and duplicate beneficiaries.
  • Swachh Bharat (2014): about 120 million household toilets built in five years; the country was declared "open defecation free" in 2019 (surveys show use lagging behind construction, but a large real change). Ujjwala (2016): over 100 million cooking-gas connections for poor women, cutting smoke from wood and dung fires (many families still cannot afford refills). Saubhagya (2017): electricity to the last 28 million unconnected households; every village was connected by April 2018. PM Awas (2015): over 40 million houses built or under construction for the poor. Jal Jeevan (2019): rural households with a tap connection rose from about 17% to about 80%. Ayushman Bharat (2018): free hospital treatment up to Rs 5 lakh a year for about 550 million people (extended to everyone over 70 in 2024). PM-KISAN (2019): Rs 6,000 a year to about 90-100 million farming families. Free grain for about 800 million people since the pandemic (extended to 2028).
  • The welfare laws it had opposed (MGNREGA, food security, Aadhaar) were kept and in some cases enlarged. Critics note that outcome measures (child stunting, anaemia, learning levels) improved more slowly than the counts of toilets and cylinders, and that public health spending stayed near 1.9% of GDP.

The economy: big reforms, one big shock, and a long recovery

  • 2014-16 The Planning Commission is replaced by NITI Aayog (an advisory body). Foreign investment is opened in defence, railways, insurance and more. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016) lets creditors take over and sell failing companies within a fixed time, for the first time in India. The Real Estate (Regulation) Act (2016) protects home-buyers. The RBI gets a legal inflation target of 4% (2016). The RBI's asset-quality review (2015) forces banks to admit their bad loans. A land acquisition ordinance to ease the 2013 law is withdrawn after a political backlash.
  • 8 November 2016 Demonetisation: at 8 pm the prime minister announces that Rs 500 and Rs 1,000 notes, 86% of all cash by value, are worthless from midnight, to attack "black money", fake currency and terror financing. Queues at banks last weeks; the informal economy, which runs on cash, seizes up; small businesses and farm markets are hit hard; at least 100 deaths are linked to the queues. The RBI later reports that 99.3% of the cancelled notes came back, meaning almost no black money was destroyed. Growth slows from 8.3% to 6.8% the next year. Supporters credit it with pushing digital payments and tax compliance; most economists regard it as a costly mistake. The Supreme Court upheld its legality 4-1 in 2023.
  • 1 July 2017 The Goods and Services Tax replaces 17 central and state taxes with one, after a constitutional amendment agreed with the states and a council in which the centre and states decide rates together. Launched with four main rates and messy software, it hurt small firms at first; it has since become the main source of indirect revenue (over Rs 22 lakh crore in 2024-25) and was simplified to two main rates (5% and 18%) in September 2025.
  • 2017-19 Bank rescue: about Rs 2.1 lakh crore of government money into public banks, mergers of 10 banks into 4, big defaulters (Essar, Bhushan, Jet Airways) sent through the bankruptcy code. The flight of Vijay Mallya (2016) and the Rs 13,000 crore Nirav Modi fraud at Punjab National Bank (2018) embarrass the government; the collapse of the infrastructure lender IL&FS (2018) sets off a credit squeeze. Growth slows to 5-6% by 2019. The corporate tax rate is cut to 22% (15% for new factories) in September 2019.
  • 2020-21 COVID: the economy shrinks 5.8% in 2020-21. A Rs 20 lakh crore "self-reliant India" package is mostly credit guarantees rather than spending. Production-Linked Incentives (Rs 1.97 lakh crore) are offered in 14 sectors to attract manufacturing. Four labour codes replacing 29 laws are passed (implemented only in November 2025). Three farm laws are passed and later repealed (see below). Air India is sold to the Tatas (2021-22); LIC is partly listed (2022).
  • 2021-26 Recovery and a capital-spending boom: the central government's investment budget rises from about Rs 2 lakh crore (2014) to Rs 11.2 lakh crore (2025-26) and Rs 12.2 lakh crore (2026-27), mainly roads, rail and ports. Growth runs 7-9% a year (9.7% in 2021-22, 7.6%, 9.2%, 7.1%, and 7.7% in 2025-26 by the provisional estimate of June 2026). Inflation stays within the RBI's band most of the time. Bad loans fall to about 2.5% of bank lending and banks post record profits. The 2025 budget exempts incomes up to Rs 12 lakh from income tax; a new simplified Income-tax Act takes effect in 2026. The 2026-27 budget sets the fiscal deficit at 4.3% of GDP and shifts the anchor to cutting public debt towards 50% of GDP by 2031.
  • 2025-26 External shocks: the United States imposes a 50% tariff on most Indian goods (August 2025), half of it as punishment for buying Russian oil; a February 2026 framework cuts the rate to 18%, then a US Supreme Court ruling scraps the legal basis for those tariffs and a temporary 10-15% tariff replaces them while a formal agreement is negotiated (still not signed as of October 2026). India concludes a free trade agreement with the European Union (January 2026) after 18 years of talks, signs one with Britain (2025), and brings one with the European Free Trade Association into force (2025). From late February 2026 the Iran war closes the Strait of Hormuz, through which half of India's crude and most of its imported cooking gas pass: domestic LPG is rationed, industrial gas use is cut, the rupee slides to about Rs 95-96 per dollar, and in October 2026 the RBI raises interest rates to 5.5% to fight oil-driven inflation (4.8% in August 2026). Diversified crude supplies (Russia, the United States, Africa) and a strategic reserve cushion the blow; growth forecasts for 2026-27 are still around 7%.

The scorecard on the economy, briefly: India went from the tenth-largest economy (2014, about $2 trillion) to the fourth (2025, about $4 trillion), overtaking Britain in 2022 and Japan in 2025; income per person roughly doubled in dollar terms; extreme poverty (World Bank $3-a-day line) fell from 27% (2011-12) to 5.3% (2022-23), with about 270 million people crossing the line; inflation averaged about 5% against about 10% in the previous five years; foreign reserves rose from $300 billion to a peak above $700 billion (2024). On the other side: private investment stayed weak for most of the decade; manufacturing's share of the economy did not rise despite "Make in India" (electronics and mobile phones being the big exceptions, with Apple now making a fifth of its iPhones in India); exports of goods grew slowly; the rupee fell from 60 to about 95 per dollar; youth unemployment stood at about 16% in mid-2026, and women's paid work, though rising in the statistics, is mostly unpaid family labour or self-employment; inequality by most measures rose, with the top 1% holding about 40% of wealth; and the credibility of official statistics was questioned when the 2021 census was postponed indefinitely (it is now scheduled for 2027), an unemployment survey was withheld in 2019, and a new GDP series was disputed.

Infrastructure and the digital state

  • National highways grew from about 91,000 km to over 146,000 km; the 1,350 km Delhi-Mumbai expressway, the Atal tunnel, the Chenab rail bridge (the world's highest, opened June 2025, completing the rail link to Kashmir), and the Mumbai-Ahmedabad bullet train (delayed to 2028-29) are the showpieces. Airports went from 74 to about 160; metro rail from 250 km in five cities to over 1,000 km in more than 20; railway electrification from 40% to nearly 100%; over 130 "Vande Bharat" semi-high-speed train services. Renewable energy capacity quadrupled; India reached 50% non-fossil power capacity in 2025, five years before its target, and set a net-zero target of 2070 (2021).
  • Digital public infrastructure became India's most admired export: the Unified Payments Interface (UPI, 2016) now handles about 20 billion transactions a month, more than Visa and Mastercard combined worldwide by count, and is now accepted in several other countries; Aadhaar covers 1.4 billion people; the CoWIN system ran the world's largest vaccination drive; DigiLocker, the ONDC commerce network and the account-aggregator system followed. Mobile data is among the cheapest in the world; internet users rose from about 250 million to over 950 million; 5G was rolled out in two years. India hosted the AI Impact Summit in February 2026.

Hindu nationalism by law

In simple words

Earlier BJP-led governments had put their core religious agenda aside to keep allies. With a majority of its own, this government did the things its movement had wanted since the 1950s: removed Kashmir's special status, built the Ram temple at Ayodhya, criminalised instant Muslim divorce, passed a citizenship law that excludes Muslims, regulated Muslim religious endowments, and backed state laws against religious conversion and interfaith marriage. Supporters see the correction of decades of "appeasement" and the restoration of Hindu civilisational pride; critics see a project to make India's 200 million Muslims second-class citizens. Both see the same facts.

  • 2015-18 Cow-protection vigilantes lynch Muslim and Dalit cattle traders and transporters (Mohammad Akhlaq in Dadri, 2015; Pehlu Khan, 2017; dozens more). The prime minister condemns the violence in general terms; some ministers honour convicted killers. The Supreme Court issues guidelines against mob lynching (2018). "Love jihad", the claim that Muslim men entrap Hindu women, becomes the basis of laws in a dozen states restricting interfaith marriage and conversion (from 2020). Beef bans tighten in several states.
  • 30 July 2019 Triple talaq (instant divorce by saying "talaq" three times), already declared invalid by the Supreme Court in 2017, is made a criminal offence for Muslim men.
  • 5 August 2019 Article 370 is hollowed out by presidential order and Jammu and Kashmir is split into two centrally run union territories (Ladakh separately), with the state's politicians jailed and the internet shut for months (the longest shutdown in any democracy). The Supreme Court upholds the move (2023). Elections in 2024 return an elected government under Omar Abdullah; the promised restoration of statehood has not yet happened. Tourism boomed until the 2025 Pahalgam attack. In Ladakh, a demand for constitutional protection of the tribal majority turned violent in September 2025 (four killed in Leh) and the activist Sonam Wangchuk was jailed under the National Security Act.
  • 9 November 2019 The Supreme Court unanimously gives the Ayodhya site to a trust for a Ram temple, calling the 1992 demolition illegal and granting Muslims five acres elsewhere. The prime minister consecrates the temple on 22 January 2024 in a nationally televised ceremony.
  • 11 December 2019 The Citizenship Amendment Act offers fast-track citizenship to Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian refugees from Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan who arrived before 2015, but not Muslims: the first time religion enters India's citizenship law. Combined with talk of a nationwide register of citizens (the Assam register of 2019 had left 1.9 million people off the list), it sets off months of protests led by Muslim women (Shaheen Bagh) and, in February 2020, communal riots in Delhi that kill 53, mostly Muslims. The rules to implement the Act are notified in March 2024.
  • 2020-25 "Bulldozer justice": state governments, led by Uttar Pradesh, demolish the homes and shops of people accused of crimes or rioting, mostly Muslims, without trial; the Supreme Court bans the practice in November 2024. Court-ordered surveys of mosques at Varanasi (Gyanvapi) and Mathura, and violence during one at Sambhal (2024), revive the Ayodhya pattern; the Supreme Court freezes new such suits (December 2024). Uttarakhand enacts the first state Uniform Civil Code (2025). The Waqf (Amendment) Act (April 2025) brings Muslim charitable endowments under tighter state control, with non-Muslim members on their boards; the Supreme Court partly stays it. No Muslim has sat in the union cabinet since 2022; the BJP has no Muslim MP; Muslim representation in the Lok Sabha (24 of 543) is among the lowest since independence.
  • 2023-26 Manipur: from May 2023 the Meitei majority and the Kuki-Zo tribal minority (largely Christian) fight an ethnic war that kills over 260 people, displaces 60,000, burns hundreds of churches and temples, and partitions the state into zones. The chief minister stays in office for 21 months; the prime minister does not visit the state; President's Rule is imposed in February 2025 and a new BJP-led government, promising Kuki and Naga deputy chief ministers, takes office in February 2026. The AFSPA remains in force in parts of the state.

Security and foreign policy

  • Pakistan: a surprise visit to Lahore (December 2015) was followed within a week by an attack on the Pathankot airbase. After the Uri attack (September 2016, 19 soldiers), India announced "surgical strikes" across the Line of Control. After the Pulwama bombing (February 2019, 40 paramilitary soldiers), Indian jets bombed a militant camp at Balakot inside Pakistan proper, the first such strike since 1971; a dogfight followed and a captured Indian pilot was returned. After the Pahalgam massacre of 26 tourists (April 2025), India put the Indus Waters Treaty "in abeyance" and launched Operation Sindoor (7-10 May 2025), missile and drone strikes on nine militant sites and then on Pakistani airbases, answered by Pakistani drones and shelling that killed civilians in Poonch; India acknowledged losing some aircraft without giving numbers; a ceasefire came on 10 May, which President Trump claimed to have brokered and India denied. The new doctrine: any major terror attack will be treated as an act of war. Talks are frozen; the water treaty remains suspended; relations in 2026 are a tense stand-off.
  • China: after years of summitry (Xi's 2014 visit, Wuhan 2018, Mamallapuram 2019) and the 73-day Doklam standoff (2017), Chinese troops moved into disputed areas of eastern Ladakh in 2020. On 15 June 2020, at Galwan, 20 Indian soldiers and an unknown number of Chinese died in hand-to-hand fighting, the first deaths on the border since 1975. India banned Chinese apps (TikTok and 200 others), restricted Chinese investment and built border roads at speed, while 50,000 troops faced each other for four years. An agreement on patrolling (October 2024) and a Modi-Xi meeting at Kazan started a thaw; direct flights resumed (2025); Xi came to Delhi for the BRICS summit in September 2026, his first visit in seven years, where both sides spoke of being "partners, not rivals" while Modi insisted border peace remains the precondition. Trade with China hit a record $151 billion in 2025-26, with India's deficit at $112 billion.
  • The United States: the partnership deepened under Obama, Trump and Biden: defence agreements (2016-20), the revived Quad with Japan and Australia (2017, leaders' summits from 2021), jet-engine and technology deals (2023), "Howdy Modi" and "Namaste Trump" rallies. Under Trump's second term the relationship was shaken by the 50% tariffs, a $100,000 fee on H-1B work visas (2025), Trump's warmth towards Pakistan's army chief, and his claims over the May 2025 ceasefire; the February 2026 trade framework began a repair that is still under way.
  • Russia and the world: India refused to condemn Russia's invasion of Ukraine, abstaining at the UN, and bought discounted Russian oil, which rose from under 2% to about 40% of its imports; Modi told Putin "this is not an era of war" (2022). India presided over the G20 in 2023 and hosted a summit that produced a joint declaration and admitted the African Union; hosted BRICS in 2026; champions a "Global South" voice; set up the International Solar Alliance and the Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure; ran large evacuations from Ukraine (2022), Sudan (2023), Israel (2023) and Iran (2025); and deepened Gulf ties (a trade pact with the UAE in 2022; nine million Indians work in the Gulf). Relations with Canada collapsed after Canada accused Indian agents of the 2023 murder of a Sikh separatist and were repaired in 2025; a US indictment alleged an Indian official plotted a similar killing in New York. Bangladesh's Sheikh Hasina, India's closest regional ally, was overthrown in August 2024 and fled to India; the Bangladesh Nationalist Party won the February 2026 election. Nepal's government fell to youth protests in 2025 and the Rastriya Swatantra Party of Kathmandu's former mayor Balendra Shah surged in the March 2026 election. The Maldives flipped from "India Out" to rapprochement (2025); Sri Lanka was rescued with $4 billion in 2022.
  • Defence: a Chief of Defence Staff was created (2020); Rafale jets (36 delivered, 26 more for the navy ordered in 2025), the aircraft carrier Vikrant (2022), the multiple-warhead Agni-V (2024); defence exports rose from under Rs 1,000 crore to about Rs 24,000 crore; the "Agnipath" four-year recruitment scheme (2022) triggered riots among job-seekers and remains contested. India is the world's second-largest arms importer and spends about 2% of GDP on defence.
  • Maoists: violence fell by more than two-thirds; the government set a target of ending the insurgency by March 2026 and, after killing most of the Maoist leadership in 2025 encounters, declared it all but over in 2026, a claim human rights groups treat with caution.

COVID-19

  • On 24 March 2020, with four hours' notice, India entered the world's strictest lockdown. Tens of millions of migrant workers, with no work, wages or transport, walked hundreds of kilometres home; hundreds died on the roads and railways before special trains were run in May. The first wave was contained at a very high cost.
  • The second wave (April-May 2021, the Delta variant) overwhelmed hospitals: oxygen ran out in Delhi and other cities, crematoria worked around the clock, bodies floated in the Ganga. The government had allowed the Kumbh Mela and held large election rallies in the weeks before. Official deaths stand at about 530,000; the World Health Organisation estimated excess deaths at about 4.7 million by the end of 2021, which the government rejected; India's own registration data later showed excess mortality in the low millions.
  • Vaccination was the recovery: two Indian-made vaccines (the Serum Institute's Covishield and Bharat Biotech's Covaxin), over 2.2 billion doses, free, tracked on CoWIN; India also exported and donated vaccines ("Vaccine Maitri") before pausing during the second wave. Free grain for 800 million people was the main welfare response.

The farm laws, 2020-21

Three laws passed by voice vote in September 2020 allowed farmers to sell outside government-regulated markets, enter contract farming, and removed stock limits on traders. Economists broadly supported the aims; farmers in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, who depend on guaranteed-price purchases, saw them as a path to corporate control and the end of the MSP system. They camped on Delhi's borders for a year, through winter and the second COVID wave; over 700 died by the unions' count; a tractor rally on Republic Day 2021 breached the Red Fort; a minister's convoy ran over protesters at Lakhimpur Kheri (October 2021). On 19 November 2021 the prime minister apologised and repealed the laws. A legal guarantee of MSP, the farmers' core demand, is still unresolved, and protests resumed in 2024.

Politics, institutions and the democracy debate

  • Elections: the BJP won 282 seats (2014) and 303 (2019), dominated state politics across the north and west, and won first-ever governments in Odisha (2024), Delhi (2025, ending the Aam Aadmi Party's decade) and West Bengal (May 2026, with 207 of 294 seats, ending Mamata Banerjee's 15 years; Suvendu Adhikari became the state's first BJP chief minister). It lost Karnataka and Telangana to Congress (2023) and failed to win Jammu and Kashmir, Jharkhand (2024), Kerala (where the Congress-led front won 102 of 140 seats in 2026 under V.D. Satheesan) or Tamil Nadu (where the actor Vijay's new party, TVK, won 108 of 234 seats in 2026 and he became chief minister, with the DMK's M.K. Stalin losing his own seat). The 2024 general election cut the BJP to 240 seats, which the opposition read as a rebuke on jobs, prices and the Constitution, and which the party answered with a sweep of Haryana, Maharashtra, Delhi, Bihar (November 2025) and Bengal.
  • Laws of state: three new criminal codes replaced the colonial-era Penal Code and procedure (in force July 2024); the Women's Reservation amendment (2023) reserves a third of seats in Parliament and state assemblies once constituencies are redrawn after the 2027 census; a law on simultaneous national and state elections ("One Nation, One Election", 2024) is with a parliamentary committee that in 2026 was still consulting states; the law on appointing Election Commissioners (2023) removed the Chief Justice from the selection panel; the Digital Personal Data Protection Act (2023) was passed; and the decision (April 2025) to count caste in the 2027 census reversed a position the government had held for a decade.
  • The money: the government's electoral bonds scheme (2018) let companies donate anonymously to parties; the Supreme Court struck it down as unconstitutional (February 2024), and the disclosed data showed the BJP had received about half of the Rs 16,500 crore donated, often from firms facing investigations or seeking contracts.
  • The agencies and the opposition: the Enforcement Directorate and the Central Bureau of Investigation pursued opposition politicians at a rate critics called selective: two sitting chief ministers (Hemant Soren and Arvind Kejriwal) were jailed in 2024, Rahul Gandhi was disqualified from Parliament for four months over a defamation conviction (2023), and many opposition figures who joined the BJP saw their cases fade. Parliament passed major laws with little debate (146 opposition MPs were suspended in a single session in December 2023); the sedition law was suspended by the Supreme Court (2022) and replaced by a similar provision in the new codes; the anti-terror UAPA was used against students, activists and journalists (several of those arrested in the Bhima Koregaon case spent years in jail without trial; the Jesuit priest Stan Swamy died in custody in 2021); the Pegasus spyware was found on opposition and journalists' phones (2021); foreign-funding licences of Amnesty, Oxfam and others were cancelled; and the BBC's offices were raided after a documentary on 2002. Reporters Without Borders ranked India around 150th of 180 countries for press freedom; the V-Dem institute reclassified India as an "electoral autocracy" (2021); the government and its supporters reject these rankings as biased.
  • Federal strain: opposition-ruled states fought the centre over Governors sitting on bills (the Supreme Court in 2025 said Governors cannot sit on bills indefinitely, then declined in an advisory opinion to set rigid timelines), over tax shares, over the three-language policy in schools, and above all over the coming delimitation, which southern states fear will shift Lok Sabha seats to the populous north.
  • 2025-26: the voter-roll fight. The Election Commission's "Special Intensive Revision" of electoral rolls, begun in Bihar before the 2025 state election and extended nationwide, removed by media counts over 130 million names as dead, duplicate, migrated or unverifiable. The opposition and a satirical youth movement, the "Cockroach Janta Party" (named in mockery of a Chief Justice's remark about critical young people), charged that genuine voters, disproportionately poor and Muslim, were being struck off to favour the ruling party; the Commission and the BJP deny this. On 10 October 2026 police locked down central Delhi, detained the movement's leaders and over 2,000 protesters, and released the leaders the next day; the movement, backed by Rahul Gandhi, demands the Chief Election Commissioner's resignation and a new way of appointing commissioners.
The two readings of Indian democracy today

The supporters' reading: elections are held on time and are decisive (the ruling party lost its majority in 2024 and lost Karnataka, Telangana, Kerala and Tamil Nadu); turnout is high; the courts strike down government schemes (electoral bonds, bulldozers, Section 66A); the media and social media are noisy and critical; the opposition governs large states; India's rankings fall because Western indices misunderstand a confident, majority-Hindu democracy correcting an elite consensus. The critics' reading: elections can be free while the field is tilted, through money (bonds), agencies (ED, CBI), media ownership, a compliant Election Commission, voter-roll purges, laws that criminalise dissent, and a Parliament that does not debate; minorities are being written out of the nation by law and by fear; institutions are not destroyed but hollowed. Where a reader stands on this is the central political question of the 2020s, and this report cannot settle it; it can only lay out the record above.

Society, science and culture

  • Caste: a 10% quota for the "economically weaker" among castes with no other reservation (2019, upheld 2022); the Supreme Court allowed states to sub-divide the Dalit quota (2024); Bihar's caste survey (2023) found backward classes to be 63% of the state; the decision to count caste nationally (2025) answered a demand the opposition had made the centre of its "social justice" campaign; the Hathras rape and secret cremation of a Dalit woman (2020), the suicide of the Dalit scholar Rohith Vemula (2016) and the flogging of Dalits at Una (2016) were flashpoints; Maratha, Jat, Patidar and Kapu agitations for quotas showed the squeeze on farming castes.
  • Women and gender: the Supreme Court decriminalised homosexuality (2018), allowed women of all ages into the Sabarimala temple (2018, review pending), gave women permanent commissions in the army (2020), recognised privacy as a fundamental right (2017), and refused to legalise same-sex marriage (2023). The 2012 Delhi rapists were hanged (2020). Olympic wrestlers protested for months (2023) against a BJP MP they accused of sexual harassment; a doctor's rape and murder at a Kolkata hospital (2024) brought nationwide protests. Girls now outnumber boys in higher education; the fertility rate is 2.0, below replacement; the sex ratio at birth has improved slightly.
  • Education and health: the National Education Policy (2020) promised mother-tongue teaching, flexible degrees and a common foundation; a leak in the 2024 medical entrance exam (NEET) affected 2.4 million candidates; literacy reached 81%, higher education enrolment about 29%; medical colleges doubled to about 780; infant mortality fell to about 26 per 1,000 and maternal deaths fell by a third; but child stunting (36%) and women's anaemia (57%) remain among the world's worst, and air pollution in north India, the worst of any populated region on earth, takes years off lives.
  • Science: Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole (23 August 2023), a first for any country; Aditya-L1 reached its station to study the Sun (2024); Shubhanshu Shukla flew to the International Space Station on a commercial mission (2025); the uncrewed test of India's own crewed spacecraft, Gaganyaan, is due in late 2026 or 2027; the Micron and Tata semiconductor plants and the first made-in-India chips (2025) began an industry; India became the "pharmacy of the world" during COVID and the second-largest mobile-phone maker. India's first Nobel-class recognition in decades remains elusive, and research spending is about 0.65% of GDP.
  • Culture and sport: cricket's T20 World Cup (2024), the Champions Trophy (2025) and, for the first time, the Women's World Cup (November 2025); Neeraj Chopra's Olympic gold (2021); D. Gukesh became the youngest world chess champion (2024); a bid for the 2036 Olympics; the Oscars for RRR's song and The Elephant Whisperers (2023); the rise of south Indian cinema over Bollywood; streaming platforms; the International Booker for Geetanjali Shree (2022) and Banu Mushtaq (2025); the Kumbh Mela of 2025, claimed as the largest gathering in history (and marred by a deadly stampede); International Yoga Day (from 2015); the Statue of Unity (2018), the world's tallest; a new Parliament building (2023); the renaming of roads, cities and institutions; and textbook revisions that removed or shortened sections on the Mughals, Gandhi's assassin and the 2002 riots. Disasters marked the decade too: the Kerala floods (2018), the Uttarakhand glacier burst (2021), the Wayanad landslides (2024), the Morbi bridge collapse (2022), the Balasore triple train crash (2023, nearly 300 dead), the Air India crash at Ahmedabad (June 2025, 260 dead), stampedes at Hathras (2024), the Kumbh, Bengaluru and Karur (2025), and a car bomb near Delhi's Red Fort (November 2025).

The ups

  • Delivery at scale: toilets, gas, electricity, houses, tap water, bank accounts, health cover, cash to farmers; extreme poverty cut to about 5%.
  • GST, the bankruptcy code, inflation targeting, the bank clean-up: structural reforms that previous governments had wanted and failed to pass.
  • Infrastructure and digital public goods (UPI, Aadhaar, CoWIN) that the world now copies.
  • Macro stability through COVID, the Ukraine war, US tariffs and the Hormuz shock; the fourth-largest economy, growing fastest among large ones.
  • Decisive responses to terror (2016, 2019, 2025) and to China's 2020 move; a larger global role (G20, Quad, Global South).
  • Chandrayaan-3; 50% clean power; the Ram temple and Article 370 as the fulfilment of long-standing promises to its voters; women's reservation passed after 27 years.
  • Political stability: twelve years of one government, three election wins, and in 2024-26 a coalition that has held.

The downs

  • Demonetisation: pain without the promised gain.
  • The COVID lockdown's migrant tragedy and the second wave's avoidable deaths; the dispute over counting them.
  • Jobs: the demographic dividend partly wasted; youth unemployment around 16%; manufacturing not transformed; inequality up.
  • Lynchings, the CAA and Delhi riots, bulldozers, Manipur: the fear and exclusion felt by Muslims, Christians and tribal minorities, and the coarsening of public speech.
  • Institutions: electoral bonds, agencies against opponents, a thinner Parliament, press freedom rankings, the voter-roll controversy, the postponed census, questioned statistics.
  • Galwan and four years of lost patrolling rights in Ladakh; the collapse of ties with Bangladesh; the tariff shock from the United States.
  • Farm laws passed without consultation and repealed after 700 deaths; Agnipath; the NEET leak; air pollution.

Could it have done better? Could it have been worse?

Could have done better

  • Skipped demonetisation and spent the political capital of 2016 on GST's design, which could have launched with two rates in 2017 instead of 2025.
  • Planned the lockdown with a week's notice, transport and cash for migrants; and prepared oxygen and avoided mass gatherings before the second wave, which experts had predicted.
  • Consulted farmers before the farm laws, as the government later admitted; the reforms were sound and the method killed them.
  • Intervened in Manipur in May 2023, visited, and replaced the chief minister within weeks rather than 21 months.
  • Put the same energy into jobs, health and education quality as into roads and digital pipes: teachers, nurses, apprenticeships, labour-intensive manufacturing, urban housing.
  • Held the 2021 census on time and published all surveys; good policy needs good numbers.
  • Condemned lynchings early and by name, kept agencies at arm's length from politics, and let Parliament debate; the majoritarian and institutional costs of the decade were choices, not necessities.
  • Managed Bangladesh and Canada with more care, and China with more transparency about what was lost in 2020.

Could have been worse

  • The pandemic could have brought a famine or a financial collapse; free grain and bank guarantees prevented both, and the vaccine programme was among the world's largest and fastest.
  • The 2019, 2020 and 2025 crises with Pakistan and China could each have become a full war; all were kept limited.
  • Fiscal populism could have followed every election; instead capital spending rose and the deficit fell.
  • The 2024 verdict could have produced an unstable coalition or an attempt to override it; the government accepted the result and governed with allies.
  • India could have been dragged into a bloc in the US-China and US-Russia confrontations; it has so far kept its freedom of action.
  • The Hormuz shock of 2026 could have produced a 1991-style crisis; reserves, diversified oil sources and a credible central bank absorbed it.

What it chose

Welfare through technology rather than through rights; infrastructure and macro-stability over labour-intensive job creation; the long-held Hindu nationalist agenda over the secular settlement of 1950; a strong centre over the states; a strong leader over strong institutions; and multi-alignment, assertiveness and self-reliance abroad. It has been rewarded by voters three times and, in 2024, warned once.

What is changing now

Three things are in motion whose outcomes will define the next decade. First, the 2027 census and the delimitation that follows: the first count in 16 years, the first caste count since 1931, and a redrawing of political power between north and south that could make or break the federal bargain. Second, the question of institutions: whether the Election Commission, the courts, the agencies and the press are seen as fair by the half of the country that did not vote for the government, a question the voter-roll protests of 2026 have made urgent. Third, jobs and energy: whether India can turn a young population into a productive one before the demographic window closes around 2045, and whether it can secure its oil and gas in a world where the Gulf can be closed for months. The Modi era has settled the question of whether India can build at speed. It has reopened the question, first answered in 1950, of what kind of nation India is building.

79 years in one view: the economy

Seen government by government, India's economic story looks like a series of crises and rescues. Seen across 79 years, it has a clear shape: thirty years of slow growth under a state-run model, a decade of cautious loosening paid for with debt, a crisis, a reform, and then thirty-five years in which India grew faster than almost any large country except China.

Six eras

  1. 1947-65: Building from nothing. Planning, dams, steel, universities. Growth about 3.8% a year, three times the colonial rate, but population growth ate half of it. The Licence Raj was born.
  2. 1965-80: Crisis and controls. Droughts, wars, the oil shock, bank nationalisation, the Emergency. Growth about 3.5% a year, the "Hindu rate". Poverty peaked around 55% in 1973-74. The one great success: the Green Revolution ended hunger as a national emergency.
  3. 1980-91: Loosening on borrowed money. Fewer licences, Maruti, telecom, computers. Growth 5.5%, but deficits above 8% of GDP and foreign debt that led to the 1991 collapse.
  4. 1991-2004: The reforms. Licences abolished, tariffs cut, the rupee freed, foreign investment welcomed, telecom and roads opened. Growth about 6%, reserves from $1 billion to $113 billion, the IT industry born.
  5. 2004-14: Boom and hangover. Three years above 9%, the fastest fall in poverty ever recorded, then inflation, scandal and paralysis. Average 7.7%.
  6. 2014-26: Delivery and shocks. GST, bankruptcy law, inflation targeting, digital payments, welfare by pipeline, a capital-spending boom; demonetisation, COVID, Ukraine, US tariffs and the Hormuz closure. Average about 6.5% including the pandemic year; 7-8% in the recovery.
In simple words

At 3.5% growth with 2.2% population growth, the average Indian's income took about 55 years to double. At 7% growth with 1% population growth, it doubles every 12 years. That is the whole difference between the India of 1980 and the India of 2026.

What went right

  • Food: from famine-prone importer to the world's largest producer of milk, pulses and spices, second-largest of rice, wheat and sugar, and a net exporter.
  • Services and software: an industry that did not exist in 1990 now exports about $200 billion a year and employs five million people directly; India's "digital public infrastructure" is a model for other countries.
  • Macroeconomic stability since 1991: no default, no hyperinflation, no banking collapse, through the Asian crisis, 2008, COVID and the shocks of 2022-26.
  • Poverty: by every measure, from roughly half the population in the 1970s to about 5% in extreme poverty (and about a quarter by the World Bank's higher $4.20 line) by 2022-23. More people have escaped poverty in India since 2005 than the entire population of the United States.
  • Pharmaceuticals, space, telecom, two-wheelers, generic drugs, vaccines: sectors where India is among the world's top three.

What is unfinished

  • Jobs. About 8 to 10 million people join the workforce every year; the economy has not created enough good jobs for them. Nearly half of all workers are still in farming, which produces a sixth of national income; 85-90% of jobs are informal; youth unemployment is about 16% and among graduates higher. India never built the labour-intensive factory sector (garments, shoes, toys, assembly) that lifted East Asia; it went from farms to services, skipping the middle.
  • Women's work. Only about four in ten women are in the workforce, and most of those in unpaid family or self-employment, against seven in ten in China. The economist's estimate is that closing this gap would add a quarter or more to national income.
  • The states have diverged. The south and west (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Kerala, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Telangana) have incomes two to three times those of Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand and Madhya Pradesh, and far better health and schooling. The populous, poorer north will gain parliamentary seats after 2027; the richer south fears paying more and counting for less.
  • Human capital. Public spending on health (about 1.9% of GDP) and education (about 4%) stays below what the government's own policies call for; a third of children are stunted; surveys show half of rural fifth-graders cannot read a second-grade text.
  • Inequality and the top. The top 1% hold about 40% of wealth and earn over 20% of income, higher than under the British Raj by one estimate; a few business houses dominate airports, ports, telecom, retail and media, and the closeness of business to government, old in India, has a new scale.
  • The slow institutions: about 50 million pending court cases; land records that are unreliable; a tax system that still produces disputes worth lakhs of crores; cities growing without plans.
The comparison that stings

In 1960 South Korea and India had roughly the same income per person. Korea educated everyone, exported everything it could make, and is now 12 times richer per person. In 1980 China and India were roughly equal; China is now about five times richer per person, mainly because it built factories that employed hundreds of millions of former farmers. India's choices between 1950 and 1990 (controls, import substitution, universities before schools) cost it those decades. Its choices since 1991 have recovered much, but not all, of the lost ground.

Society: health, education, women, caste, cities

People

India had about 340 million people in 1947 and has about 1.46 billion now: it added a Europe, a United States and a Brazil. The population grew fastest (over 2% a year) between 1961 and 1991, as deaths fell faster than births. Family planning was a failure under coercion (the Emergency) and a slow success under development: as girls stayed in school and infant deaths fell, families shrank. The average woman had about six children in 1950 and has two today, below the replacement level; the south reached this twenty years before the north. India overtook China as the most populous country in 2023, and its population will peak around 1.7 billion in the 2060s. For now it is young: the median Indian is 29 (China 40, Japan 49), which is the opportunity and the test of the next twenty years.

Health

A child born in 1947 could expect to live about 32 years; one born today, about 72. Of every 1,000 babies born in 1951, about 146 died in their first year; today about 26 do. Smallpox was eradicated (India's last case was in 1975), polio (last case 2011), and malaria, which infected 75 million a year, now infects a few hundred thousand. The maternal death rate fell from about 400 per 100,000 births (2000) to about 90. The rural health mission put a health worker in nearly every village; a national insurance scheme now covers half a billion people for hospital care. The failures: a third of children stunted and more than half of women anaemic; public health spending still under 2% of GDP, so most families pay out of pocket; and the catastrophe of COVID's second wave. Education

In 1951, 18 of 100 Indians could read; today 81 can. Among women the change is from 9 to about 75. The average adult had less than a year of schooling in 1950 and has about seven years now. Nearly every child enrols in primary school (the Right to Education Act of 2009 made it a legal duty), about 29% of young adults enter college, and India produces more engineers than any country except China. Nehru's IITs and the later IIMs, AIIMS and national labs became global brands. The failure is quality: the annual ASER surveys find that about half of rural children in the fifth grade cannot read a second-grade text or do simple division, and the 2020 education policy's promise of early-grade literacy is still a promise. The learning gap between rich and poor, and between private and government schools, is the engine of inequality in the next generation.

Women

  • 1950-56 Equal vote; the Hindu Code gives women rights in marriage, divorce and inheritance.
  • 1961 Dowry outlawed (and still paid in most marriages).
  • 1974-83 The "Towards Equality" report; the women's movement; rape law reformed after the Mathura case; anti-dowry and anti-sati laws after Delhi bride-burnings and Roop Kanwar.
  • 1993 One-third of village and city council seats reserved: over a million women elected.
  • 1997-2013 Supreme Court guidelines on workplace harassment (Vishaka); the Domestic Violence Act (2005); after the 2012 Delhi gang rape, a sweeping new criminal law.
  • 2017-23 Instant divorce criminalised; 26 weeks' maternity leave; women in combat roles and the military academy; one-third of Parliament reserved (to take effect after 2027).

What the numbers say: child marriage fell from about half of all girls in the 1990s to about a quarter; girls now outnumber boys in college; the sex ratio at birth, distorted by sex-selective abortion since the 1980s, has improved slightly. What they also say: fewer than one woman in ten owns land; about one in three has suffered violence from a husband; only 14% of MPs are women; and paid work for women remains among the lowest in the world. A woman has been prime minister (1966), President (2007), and chief minister in a dozen states, and women now vote at higher rates than men; parties compete for them with cash transfers. The gap between legal equality and lived equality is the story.

Caste

The Constitution abolished untouchability and reserved places for the lowest castes; 76 years later the reservations have created a Dalit and tribal middle class, Dalit presidents and chief ministers, and a political arithmetic no party can ignore. Mandal did the same for the middle castes. Atrocities against Dalits are still recorded at about 50,000 a year and often go unpunished; manual scavenging persists despite bans; fewer than 6% of marriages cross caste lines; and caste discrimination has followed Indians into cities, universities and the diaspora. The 2027 census will, for the first time since 1931, count how many people belong to which caste, and the fight over what to do with those numbers (quotas within quotas, a higher ceiling, a share for the dominant farming castes) will define the next decade of social politics.

Cities and migration

Seventeen of every 100 Indians lived in towns in 1951; about 36 do now, and the real figure, counting the villages that have become suburbs, is higher. Mumbai, Delhi, Kolkata, Bengaluru, Chennai and Hyderabad are among the 30 largest urban areas on earth; about 450 million Indians are internal migrants; and nine million work in the Gulf, sending home more money (about $135 billion a year, the most of any country) than foreign investors bring. The cities are the engines of growth and the sites of its failures: slums (about a sixth of the urban population), the world's worst air, floods every monsoon, and the 2020 lockdown's exodus that showed how little the cities had done for the people who built them.

Religion and communal relations

India is about 80% Hindu, 14% Muslim (about 200 million people, the third-largest Muslim population in the world), 2.3% Christian, 1.7% Sikh, with Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, Jews and tribal faiths. How these communities live together has been the deepest question of the republic since the day it was born in a religious massacre.

The arc

  • 1947-64: After Partition and Gandhi's murder, the founders chose a state with no religion and equal citizenship, reformed Hindu law by statute, and left Muslim personal law alone. Communal violence was low. The RSS and the Jana Sangh were small.
  • 1964-84: Riots returned (Ahmedabad 1969, Moradabad 1980, Nellie 1983); Sikh politics turned violent in Punjab; the Meenakshipuram conversions (1981) and the VHP's campaigns began mass Hindu mobilisation; 1984 brought Blue Star and the anti-Sikh pogrom.
  • 1984-92: Shah Bano, the Ayodhya locks, the Rath Yatra, the demolition: the Ram temple movement took the BJP from 2 seats to 120 and made religion the axis of north Indian politics.
  • 1992-2014: Mumbai 1992-93, Gujarat 2002, Kandhamal 2008, Muzaffarnagar 2013; a wave of Islamist terrorism (1993, 2001, 2006, 2008); the Sachar Committee's finding that Muslims had fallen behind Dalits; the BJP's first national government, which shelved its religious agenda to keep allies.
  • 2014-26: The religious agenda enacted: Article 370, the temple, triple talaq, the citizenship law, the Waqf law, state conversion and "love jihad" laws, bulldozers, lynchings; Muslim representation at its lowest; large riots rarer but a steady hum of smaller violence and hate speech; Manipur's ethnic-religious war; the Sikh diaspora's Khalistan activism abroad.

What the record shows

  • Large-scale communal massacres have become rarer since 2002: no event since has approached Gujarat's toll, and the 1980s-90s peak of several hundred riots a year has fallen. What has risen is everyday pressure: lynching, discrimination in housing and jobs, demolitions, surveillance, and a political culture in which a community of 200 million has almost no voice in government.
  • Every community has grievances the state has not addressed: the Kashmiri Pandits' exile (1990), the Sikh killings of 1984 (convictions after 34 years), Hashimpura (31 years), the Gujarat survivors, the Bhagalpur and Nellie victims (never), the Christian victims of Kandhamal, the Muslims of Delhi 2020, the Kuki and Meitei dead of Manipur. Justice delayed has been the rule.
  • Hinduism itself changed: from a religion practised locally and diversely to one with a national political voice, television epics, mega-temples and mass pilgrimages; the Kumbh of 2025 drew hundreds of millions. Dalit assertion within and outside Hinduism (Ambedkar's Buddhism, the BSP), Hindu-Muslim shared shrines, and the syncretic traditions of Bengal, Punjab, Kerala and the south complicate the picture of two blocks.
  • The Supreme Court became the referee: upholding reservations for Muslims' backward sections, striking triple talaq, deciding Ayodhya, protecting Sabarimala's women, banning bulldozer demolitions, freezing new mosque suits, staying parts of the Waqf law. Both sides now see the court as the last word and fight over its composition.
The argument, fairly stated

One view: India is a civilisation that is Hindu in its soul; its secular constitution should protect minorities but not pretend the majority has no culture; the post-1947 elite "appeased" Muslim conservatives (Shah Bano), tolerated separatism in Kashmir, and sneered at Hindu belief; the corrections since 2014 are democratic, voted for, and overdue. The other view: India was founded on equal citizenship precisely because Partition showed where religious nationalism leads; a state that ranks citizens by faith, however popular, is not the republic of 1950; and a 200-million minority that is frightened is a danger to everyone's future. The reader will find both voices in Indian newspapers every morning. What this report can add is that the founders' choice was a choice, made against the mood of 1947, and that India's forty years of relative communal peace (1950s-70s) coincided with its weakest economy, while its communal turbulence (1980s-2020s) coincided with its rise. The two are not simply cause and effect, but they are the same country.

Politics and institutions

Five party systems

  1. 1952-67: One dominant party. Congress won every national election and nearly every state, with under half the vote, against a split opposition. Politics happened inside Congress.
  2. 1967-89: Congress declines, then centralises. Regional and caste parties took states; Indira Gandhi turned Congress into a family firm; the Emergency and the Janata interlude showed democracy could punish and recover.
  3. 1989-2014: The coalition era. No party won a majority for 25 years; seven prime ministers in ten years (1989-99); Mandal brought OBC parties, Ayodhya brought the BJP, regional parties became kingmakers; governments fell on a single vote or a single ally.
  4. 2014-24: BJP dominance. Two single-party majorities, the first since 1984; the BJP replaced Congress as the "natural party of government", with a social base of upper castes, OBCs, many Dalits and tribals, and Hindus of all regions except the south.
  5. 2024-: Coalition with a dominant party. The BJP needs allies at the centre but keeps winning states; the opposition is a bloc of regional parties with a weakened Congress at its core; new regional forces (a film star's party sweeping Tamil Nadu in 2026) keep appearing.

What held and what frayed

What held

  • 18 general elections, every one on time (the only delay was the Emergency's one-year extension), with power changing hands peacefully every time it was lost, including in 1977, 1989, 2004 and in dozens of states.
  • Turnout rose from 45% (1952) to 66-67% (2019, 2024); the poor, women and Dalits vote at rates equal to or higher than the rich, the reverse of most democracies.
  • The army has never interfered in politics.
  • The Supreme Court's basic structure doctrine (1973) and the 44th Amendment (1978) made a second Emergency practically impossible.
  • Federalism absorbed every separatist pressure except Kashmir's: linguistic states (1956), the Northeast's accords, three new states in 2000, Telangana in 2014.
  • Panchayati Raj gave three million elected local representatives, over a million of them women.
  • The Election Commission made the world's largest elections credible; electronic voting ended ballot-box stuffing.

What frayed

  • Parties became family businesses: the Nehru-Gandhis, and the Yadavs, Badals, Abdullahs, Karunanidhis, Pawars, Thackerays, Reddys and dozens more.
  • Crime in politics: 46% of MPs elected in 2024 face criminal cases, 31% serious ones (2004: 24%).
  • Money: elections cost an estimated $8-10 billion in 2019 and more in 2024, mostly unaccounted; electoral bonds legalised secrecy until struck down.
  • President's Rule was used over 130 times, mostly to remove opponents, until the courts restrained it in 1994; Governors remain partisan.
  • Parliament debates less: sittings fell from about 130 days a year in the 1950s to about 55-60; major laws pass by voice vote or ordinance.
  • Investigating agencies have been used against opponents by every government with the power to do so; the scale in the 2020s is the subject of the current dispute.
  • Corruption moved from the licence counter to the contract and the land deal; India ranks around 96th of 180 on Transparency International's index; the Lokpal, 50 years in the making, has made little difference.
  • The courts are overloaded (about 50 million pending cases) and the police unreformed (the 2006 Supreme Court directions are largely ignored).

Centre and states

The Constitution tilted power to the centre, and the centre has used it: the Planning Commission's money, Article 356, Governors, and since 2017 the GST Council (where the centre has a veto). The counter-forces are real: regional parties, the Finance Commissions that hand states about 41% of central taxes, the courts, and the simple fact that states run police, schools, hospitals and land. The fights of the 2020s (Governors sitting on bills, the three-language policy, cess revenues the centre keeps, the fear of delimitation) are old fights in new clothes. The constitutional freeze on Lok Sabha seats (since 1976) ends after the 2027 census; redrawing seats by population would reward Uttar Pradesh and Bihar and punish Tamil Nadu and Kerala, which did what the country asked and controlled their populations. How this is settled will test the union more than anything since 1956.

India and the world

Six phases

  1. 1947-62: Moral power. Non-alignment, Panchsheel, peacekeeping, Bandung; a voice larger than India's strength, until China showed the limits.
  2. 1962-71: Learning realism. Rearmament, the 1965 war, American wheat with strings, the Soviet treaty, the Bangladesh victory.
  3. 1971-91: Regional power, global isolation. Dominance in South Asia (Sikkim, Sri Lanka, the Maldives), the nuclear test and sanctions, a Soviet tilt, an economy too closed to matter to anyone.
  4. 1991-2004: Reorientation. Look East, Israel, the WTO, the nuclear tests and the US rapprochement, Kargil, "natural allies".
  5. 2004-14: Arrival. The nuclear deal, the G20, BRICS, 9% growth, Mumbai 2008 and restraint, a China that overtook India by a factor of five.
  6. 2014-26: Multi-alignment. Quad with the United States and Japan, BRICS and SCO with China and Russia, Russian oil and American jet engines, the G20 presidency, the "Global South", trade deals with Europe, strikes on Pakistan, a standoff and a thaw with China, tariff wars and a trade truce with Washington, and a seat at every table but no permanent seat at the Security Council.

The relationships

  • Pakistan: four wars, two near-wars, three decades of proxy terrorism, and brief peace windows (Lahore 1999, the 2004-07 back-channel, 2015). Both sides have nuclear weapons since 1998. In 2026 there is no dialogue, no trade, no treaty on the rivers, and a doctrine that the next major attack will be met with war.
  • China: from "bhai-bhai" to 1962 to a 30-year managed peace to Galwan to a cautious thaw. India depends on China for electronics, drugs' raw materials and machinery (a $112 billion deficit) while treating it as the main security threat; the border is undefined and militarised; the two countries' economies are now five to one.
  • The United States: estranged for 50 years (Pakistan, the Soviet tilt, nuclear sanctions), then partners for 25 (Clinton 2000, the nuclear deal 2008, defence pacts, the Quad), then shaken by tariffs and Trump's Pakistan tilt in 2025, now repairing. The Indian diaspora in America (five million) and Indian-origin CEOs of Google, Microsoft and IBM are its bridge.
  • Russia: the one constant: arms (still over a third of India's imports), the 1971 treaty, vetoes on Kashmir, and since 2022 discounted oil. India's refusal to condemn the Ukraine invasion cost it in Europe and bought it a seat at the Russia-China table.
  • Neighbours: India is the elephant in a room of nervous neighbours; each (Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, the Maldives, Bhutan, Myanmar) has swung between India and China. Bangladesh's turn after 2024 and the youth revolts of 2024-25 in Dhaka and Kathmandu showed India's limited control.
  • The Gulf, Israel, Europe: nine million workers and most of India's oil come from the Gulf, which has made India close to the UAE and Saudi Arabia and quiet on Palestine; Israel is a top arms supplier since 1992; Europe is the newest trade partner (the 2026 agreement). The 2026 Hormuz closure showed how much of India's prosperity floats through one strait.
  • Soft power and hard power: yoga, cinema, cricket, cuisine, the diaspora of 35 million; the third-largest defence budget, a nuclear triad, a two-carrier navy, the fourth-largest air force; and a 75-year-old demand for a permanent Security Council seat that every major power endorses and none delivers.

Culture, sport and science

Culture

Indian culture since 1947 has been both the state's project and its escape from the state. The state built the academies, the film institutes, All India Radio and Doordarshan, and used them for "national integration"; the people made Hindi cinema the largest film industry on earth (about 1,500-2,000 films a year in 20 languages), made cricket a religion, and turned Indian English writing (Rushdie, Seth, Roy, Ghosh, Desai, Adiga) and regional literatures into a world literature. Each era had its mood: the hopeful 1950s (Raj Kapoor, Satyajit Ray), the angry 1970s (Amitabh Bachchan), the consumer 1990s (the big family films, satellite TV), the global 2000s (Oscars, Bookers, the IPL), and the assertive 2010s-20s (south Indian blockbusters, streaming series, a cultural politics of temples, renaming and textbooks). Twenty-two languages are recognised by the Constitution; Hindi is the first language of about 44% and English the working language of the elite and the courts; the compromise of 1965 still holds, and still gets tested.

Sport

Hockey gave India eight Olympic golds (1928-80) and then faded; cricket gave it the 1983, 2011 and 2024 World Cups, the 2025 women's title, and the richest league in the sport. India's Olympic record is poor for a country its size (41 medals in 100 years, seven at Tokyo 2020, six at Paris 2024, with Neeraj Chopra's javelin gold the first in athletics), but badminton, wrestling, boxing, shooting and chess (Anand's five world titles, Gukesh's in 2024 at 18) have produced world champions, and a bid for the 2036 Olympics is on the table.

Science and technology

From Bhabha's reactor and Sarabhai's bicycle-borne rocket to the 1974 and 1998 tests, Chandrayaan-3's lunar landing (2023), Mars at a tenth of NASA's cost (2014), a nuclear triad, the "pharmacy of the world", the software industry and the digital public goods, India's scientific state has been one of its clearest successes. The failures are also clear: research spending stuck around 0.65% of GDP (China 2.4%); universities absent from the world's top 100; no science Nobel to a scientist working in India since C.V. Raman (1930), though Indian-origin scientists abroad (Khorana, Chandrasekhar, Ramakrishnan, Banerjee) have won; a weak link between labs and factories; and a brain drain that the diaspora's success both proves and softens.

Environment and disasters

India industrialised late and is paying the environmental bill early. Its forest cover (about 21-24% of land, depending on the definition) has roughly held since the 1980s, with quality falling; its tiger population recovered from about 1,400 (2006) to about 3,700 (2022), the world's largest; the Ganga clean-up, begun in 1986, is still unfinished. Thirteen of the world's twenty most polluted cities are in north India; Delhi's winter air shortens lives by years. Groundwater, the Green Revolution's hidden cost, is collapsing in Punjab, Haryana and the dry south; Chennai (2019) and Bengaluru (2024) have had "day zero" water crises. Heatwaves, erratic monsoons, Himalayan floods (Uttarakhand 2013 and 2021), coastal cyclones (Odisha 1999, Amphan 2020) and landslides (Wayanad 2024) are becoming more frequent. India's climate position has moved from "the rich caused this" (Indira Gandhi, 1972) to a net-zero pledge for 2070, half its power capacity from non-fossil sources (achieved 2025), and the world's cheapest solar power, while coal still generates about 70% of its electricity and new coal plants are being built.

The big what-ifs: where India could have gone another way

History does not run experiments, but asking what else was possible is how we judge choices. For each turning point, here is the case that India could have done better, the case that it could have done worse, and a verdict on what the choice cost or saved. These are judgments, not facts, and serious people disagree with every one of them.

1. What if India had put primary schools before steel plants (1950s)?

The strongest "could have done better" in this report. Taiwan, Korea, and later China reached near-universal literacy within a generation of starting; India took sixty years. An educated workforce would have made every later reform work faster and spread its gains wider. The counter-argument: the money was tiny and the dams and steel were real; the real failure was not the choice of 1950 but the failure to correct it in 1960, 1970 and 1980.

2. What if the Licence Raj had ended in 1965, or 1975, or 1985?

Each decade of delay cost perhaps 1.5-2 percentage points of growth a year, compounded. Had India opened in 1965 like Korea, it might be a middle-income country today. The counter: in the 1960s there was no business class ready to compete, no world market as open as 1991's, and the first attempt (the 1966 devaluation) failed so badly that it set reform back 25 years. Rajiv Gandhi's 1985 attempt, with 400 seats, is the real missed chance.

3. What if Nehru had not taken Kashmir to the UN, or had settled in 1953?

Most historians think a settlement along the ceasefire line was possible in the early 1950s, and that internationalising the dispute froze it. The counter: Pakistan's army, from 1958 on, had no interest in settling; the 1972 Simla window closed from Pakistan's side; and the only alternative in 1948 was a war to the Indus that India could not afford.

4. What if India had prepared for China in 1959-62?

A modernised army and a less provocative "forward policy" could have avoided the rout. The counter: China would still have kept Aksai Chin, and the defeat's shock produced the rearmament that won in 1965 and 1971.

5. What if the Emergency had never happened?

Had Indira Gandhi resigned in June 1975, India would have been spared 21 months of dictatorship, 8 million forced sterilisations and the Turkman Gate demolitions, and she might have returned stronger. The counter, uncomfortable but real: the Emergency's defeat in 1977 vaccinated India against authoritarianism more effectively than any amount of theory, and the 44th Amendment exists because of it.

6. What if Rajiv Gandhi had let the Shah Bano judgment stand and kept the Ayodhya locks shut (1986)?

The BJP's "appeasement" charge would have had less force, and the Ayodhya movement less momentum. The counter: the VHP's campaign had begun in 1984 before either decision; the demolition had many fathers; and reformist Muslim opinion was weaker than reformers believed.

7. What if Mandal had been implemented differently (1990)?

Done through consultation, with a creamy-layer rule and a matching push on schooling, it would have cost fewer lives and less bitterness. The counter: no government had dared for ten years, and the "wrong" way may have been the only way; the OBC rise it produced is one of the republic's great democratic shifts.

8. What if the Babri Masjid had been protected on 6 December 1992?

Central rule in Uttar Pradesh in November 1992 might have prevented the demolition, the riots, the Bombay blasts and, arguably, the three decades of Hindu-Muslim polarisation that followed. The counter: the movement had a million people behind it; a shooting at Ayodhya might have caused a civil war; and the court-ordered outcome of 2019 shows how much of the energy would have found a path anyway.

9. What if UPA-II had kept the fiscal discipline of 2007 and auctioned everything?

No 10% inflation, no scandals, no paralysis, GST in 2011, and perhaps a Congress still able to win 150 seats. The counter: the 2008 crisis required stimulus, and the coalition would not have allowed clean allocations in the DMK's ministries.

10. What if demonetisation had been skipped (2016)?

Almost every economist's answer: India would have had 1-2% more growth in 2017, fewer small-business failures, and GST would have had a calmer launch; digital payments were rising anyway. The counter, offered by the government: it was a signal that the rules had changed, and it widened the tax base.

11. What if the COVID lockdown had been planned (2020)?

A week's notice, transport and cash for migrants, and a staggered approach would have saved lives and dignity; preparation for a second wave would have saved far more. The counter: no country got COVID right, India's vaccine programme was among the best, and its economy recovered faster than most.

12. What if Partition had been avoided, or delayed?

The largest what-if of all. A united, federal India of 1.9 billion people would be a different civilisation and perhaps a less governable one; a delay might have reduced the killings or produced a civil war. Nobody knows. What is knowable is that Partition fixed India's choices on secularism, on the army, on Kashmir and on the Muslim question in ways that still bind it.

The balance sheet of 79 years

What India got right

  • It stayed a democracy. Of all the countries freed after 1945, almost none kept continuous competitive elections, civilian rule and a free (if pressured) press for 79 years. The one lapse lasted 21 months and was ended by voters.
  • It stayed one country, with 22 languages, every religion, and a dozen separatist movements, most settled by accords rather than only by force.
  • It fed itself and ended famine as a feature of Indian life.
  • It cut extreme poverty from half the population to about 5%, most of it in the last twenty years.
  • It doubled lifespans, quadrupled literacy, and brought infant deaths down by more than 80%.
  • It built institutions that outlast governments: the Constitution, the courts, the Election Commission, the RBI, the IITs, the scientific agencies.
  • It became a power without becoming a bully or a client: nuclear, non-aligned, and now the fourth-largest economy, with a voice in every forum.
  • It empowered the bottom of the caste order through reservation and the ballot in a way no other hierarchical society has attempted.

What it got wrong, or late

  • Thirty years of economic self-harm (1960-90) that left India two to three decades behind East Asia.
  • Schools that teach too little, and health systems that families must pay for themselves.
  • Jobs: a workforce that moved from farms to the street corner, not the factory.
  • Women's safety and women's work.
  • Religious violence and the failure to punish it, from Nellie and Delhi 1984 to Gujarat 2002 and Manipur 2023, and a political culture that now organises around religious identity.
  • Kashmir and the Northeast, where the republic's promises were repeatedly broken and the army repeatedly sent in.
  • Corruption and crime in politics, dynasties, and courts that take decades.
  • Air, water and soil damaged faster than they are being repaired.
  • Statistics and transparency that have weakened just as the country needs them most.

The road ahead

The government's stated goal is "Viksit Bharat": a developed country by 2047, the centenary of independence. That means an income per person of roughly $14,000-20,000, which requires growth of about 8% a year for two decades and, more importantly, growth that reaches the half of the population that still farms, sells, cleans and builds without contracts or security. Whether India gets there depends on six tests that every chapter of this report has pointed to.

  1. Jobs before the window closes. India's working-age population will stop growing around 2045. Either the next twenty years create 150 million productive jobs, in factories, construction, care, logistics and services that need skills, or the demographic dividend becomes a demographic grievance. This is the test that 1950-90 failed and 1991-2026 only half passed.
  2. Institutions that both halves trust. Elections, courts, the Election Commission, agencies, statistics and the press must be seen as fair by the losers as well as the winners. The voter-roll protests of 2026 are the current form of a question as old as 1951.
  3. A federal bargain for the census and delimitation. Redrawing power between north and south after 2027 without breaking the union will take the statesmanship of 1956.
  4. Social peace. A country where 200 million Muslims, 30 million Christians, 25 million Sikhs, 100 million tribal people and 200 million Dalits feel like full citizens is the only kind that can be developed in any sense that matters. The founders thought so; the argument about whether they were right is now the centre of politics.
  5. Energy, water and climate. The 2026 Hormuz closure showed the cost of importing 85% of oil; the heatwaves, failing groundwater and poisoned air show the cost of growing first and cleaning later. India has the cheapest solar power on earth and 70% coal electricity; the next decade decides which wins.
  6. The neighbourhood and China. A hostile Pakistan, an unstable Bangladesh and Nepal, and a China five times larger and on the border: India will need the restraint of 1999 and 2008, the resolve of 2020 and 2025, and more friends than it has yet made.
Two ways to read 79 years

The pessimist sees a country that wasted its first forty years, is wasting its demographic moment, is turning on its minorities, is hollowing its institutions, and will grow old before it grows rich. The optimist sees a country that was given almost no chance in 1947, that has disproved every prediction of collapse, that has moved 300 million people out of poverty in a generation, that has never had a coup or a famine, that argues ferociously and votes decisively, and that has, by any historical standard, done the hardest thing of all: it has stayed free, stayed one, and gotten steadily better. Both are looking at the same facts. The chapters above are the facts; the reader decides.
